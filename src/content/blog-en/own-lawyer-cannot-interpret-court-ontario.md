Yes — but you have to apply in advance. The court will arrange an interpreter for the [hearing](/en/family-law/divorce-litigation/), but interpreters are not available on demand: if none can be found on the day, the hearing will be adjourned and the judge will order that an interpreter be provided for the next hearing. Your own lawyer cannot act as your interpreter in court. If you want to understand what is said at the hearing, apply to the court for an interpreter in advance — or find an interpreter yourself and bring one to court.

A client came to consult me. She had hired a lawyer, but her English was poor. On the day of the hearing, she sat in the courtroom while the judge and the other side's lawyer spoke, and she understood little of what was said. Her question: will the court provide me with an interpreter? My own lawyer speaks Chinese — can't she interpret for me at the hearing?

The short answer: the court will provide an interpreter, but only if you apply in advance; your own lawyer cannot interpret for you.

---

## 1. The Court Will Arrange an Interpreter for the Hearing — but It Needs Advance Notice

At the hearing, the court will do its best to provide an interpreter. But interpreters are not available on demand: if no interpreter can be found on the day, the hearing cannot proceed and will be adjourned, and the judge will make an order that an interpreter is required for the next hearing. So the key is to apply in advance: once the court knows you need an interpreter, it can arrange one ahead of time, and the hearing goes ahead as scheduled. A person without a lawyer can ask duty counsel for help with the request; once you have a lawyer, the duty counsel route is not available to you — the request is for you and your lawyer to make.

---

## 2. Your Own Lawyer Cannot Interpret for You on the Spot

Your lawyer's role in court is to speak for you and to deal with the judge and the other side's lawyer. She cannot be your interpreter at the same time. Everything said in court is on the record; interpretation has to be accurate and neutral, and your lawyer is your advocate — one person cannot hold both roles. The judge will not allow it. However good her Chinese is, she cannot sit beside you in court translating sentence by sentence.

---

## 3. If You Don't Have a Court Order for a Court-Provided Interpreter, Hire One Yourself

As a rule, the court provides a free interpreter only to people on Legal Aid. If you are not on Legal Aid, do not expect the court to provide one free of charge. The most reliable course is to hire a court interpreter yourself: book one in advance, and have her come to court with you on the day, sit beside you, interpret what the judge and the other side say for you, and interpret what you say for the court. You pay the cost. This is not money to save — sitting through a hearing without understanding what is being said means having your affairs decided while you do not know what is happening.

---

## 4. Getting the Court to Provide an Interpreter Requires an Order from the Judge

There are two ways to get it. One is to apply in advance, by a Form 14B motion, asking for an order and setting out that you need an interpreter and in which language. The other is to raise it at your first hearing, in front of the judge, and ask the judge to make the order. Once the order is made, the court knows an interpreter is required for every hearing that follows — you do not have to raise it again each time. Whichever route you take, discuss it with your lawyer first, so that she can put the request to the court for you and explain your level of English and why you need an interpreter.

---

## FAQs

### 1. I understand a little English. Do I really need an interpreter?

Everyday English and courtroom English are two different things. What is said in court is legal terminology and procedural language, spoken fast by the judge. Understanding half of it is more dangerous than understanding none of it — you believe you understood, when you may have misunderstood. If there is any uncertainty, you need an interpreter.

### 2. Can I bring a friend or a family member who speaks English to interpret for me?

Generally, no. Court interpretation has to be neutral and accurate, and a friend or family member has an interest in your case — the court will not accept that. If you need an interpreter, hire a professional court interpreter.

### 3. If I hire an interpreter myself, how is the fee worked out?

By time and by the interpreter's qualifications — usually hourly, or by half day or full day. You pay the cost. Ask about the price and the time in advance, and get it in writing. Do not leave it until the day of the hearing to find someone.
