It is not enough that the amount was sufficient, or that the money arrived quickly. When parents' money goes into their child's own bank account, and some of it is spent on living expenses along the way, the other side will say in divorce court: that was a [gift](/en/blog/chinese-divorce-property-gift-loan-trust-ontario/). Whether the house can be kept out of the division does not turn on whether the money was enough. It turns on whether a judge can be persuaded that the money was never the child's, and that the parents stayed in control of it all along.

---

## 1. Enough Money and Fast Arrival Do Not Prove It Was Not a Gift

People in this situation usually offer two arguments. The first is that the money their parents sent added up to more than the price of the house. The house cost a few hundred thousand, and the parents sent even more than that — surely that proves the house belongs to the parents? The second is that the money went out almost as soon as it came in, within a week or two, to pay for the house. Surely that shows it was earmarked for the purchase?

Neither argument is enough in court.

Take the amount first. A large amount proves only that the parents gave the child a lot of money. A gift can be large, too. Parents giving a child more than the price of a house is entirely common. The size of the transfer says nothing about what kind of transfer it was.

Then take the speed. A week or two sounds fast, but it is not the same day. It is not a case of the parents sending the money today and the child paying it to the seller tomorrow, with the money untouched in between. In this kind of case, the money sits in the child's account for a week or two, and during those weeks the child is living a normal life. Normal life costs money. A judge who has heard these cases for years knows exactly what two weeks of ordinary living costs. A couple of thousand dollars is unavoidable. And that money came out of the very fund the child now says was never theirs.

---

## 2. The Only Question That Matters: Whose Money Was It

When the case reaches court, the judge is really asking one question: when parents transferred the money into their child's account, what was it? Did the parents intend to give the money to the child, or did they intend to hand it to the child to look after for them?

Those two answers lead to completely different outcomes. If it was a gift, the money became the child's the moment it arrived. The child could spend it however they liked, the house bought with it is the child's, and on divorce it goes into the division. If the child was holding it for the parents, the money stayed the parents' from start to finish, the house bought with it is theirs, and it should not be divided with the spouse.

And how does a judge tell the difference? By control. If the money belonged to the parents, did the child need their say-so before spending it? Was there an arrangement between the parents and the child? For example, did any agreement say how much of it the child could use for living expenses, and how much of it absolutely could not be touched? If there was no arrangement at all, and once the money landed in the child's account the child spent it as they pleased, then in the judge's eyes there is very little separating that from a gift.

So the fight is not about the amount, and it is not about the speed. It is about control: whether the parents kept a hand on the money, and whether the child treated it as their own.

There is one more point about intent that deserves to be singled out: whether the money was designated. When the parents gave the money, did they designate it for buying the house? That designation matters a great deal in judging intent. If the parents made it clear at the time that the money was for the purchase, and the child did in fact use it to pay for the house, then the intent is clear: the money had a designated purpose, and it was not given for the child to spend freely. On the other hand, if the parents said nothing when they gave the money, and the child spent it as they pleased afterwards, it is very hard for a judge to believe the money was designated for anything. Whether it was designated is judged by what the parents said and did when they gave it: whether any agreement stated the purpose, whether the money was given around the time of the purchase, and whether it was actually used for the price. Taken together, those facts show what the parents truly intended at the time.

## 3. Paying the Lawyer Directly and Paying Into the Child's Account Are Completely Different Things

This is a distinction many parents do not appreciate at the start, and it makes an enormous difference.

If the parents pay the closing lawyer directly, the case is simple. The money never passes through the child's hands at all. It goes from the parents' account to the lawyer's trust account, and then to the seller. The parents can say plainly: this was our investment, we paid for it ourselves, and it has nothing to do with our child. The child has nothing to explain, because the money never became the child's money.

But when the parents put the money into the child's own account, everything changes. The moment the money lands in that account, the bank records show it as the child's money. When the child then uses it to pay for the house, what the judge sees is: parents gave the child money, and the child bought a house with their own money. Where the child is the one who paid, and there is no agreement between the parents and the child, the parents' only route to claiming the house is a [resulting trust](/en/blog/resulting-trust-parental-gifts-ontario/) — that is, the child holds the house in name, but in reality holds it for the parents. And the decisive test for a resulting trust is what the parents intended when they gave the money: did they mean to gift it to the child, or did they mean to keep the beneficial interest for themselves? The Supreme Court of Canada said exactly this in [Pecore v. Pecore, 2007 SCC 17](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2007/2007scc17/2007scc17.html): when parents gratuitously transfer property to an adult child, the law presumes a resulting trust, and it is for the child to produce evidence that the parents truly intended a gift in order to rebut it. So in court, the fight still comes back to what the parents actually intended at the time — and with the money having passed through the child's account and living expenses having been spent out of it, the parents have to explain clearly why it can still count as their money.

It gets worse when the child has only one account. The purchase money goes in, and the living expenses come out of the same account. Once the two are [mixed together](/en/blog/mom-gift-moved-between-accounts-divorce-ontario/), no one can separate which dollar was the house money and which dollar was the grocery money. By the time of the divorce, all the other side's lawyer has to do is put the bank statements in front of the judge and ask: who spent these living expenses? And the case has already become difficult.

---

## 4. What Evidence Actually Works

The strongest evidence is a written agreement, signed before the purchase money is paid. If the parents and the child sign an agreement before payment, stating that the money belongs to the parents, that the child is holding it for them, and that the house bought with it belongs to the parents, that is an express trust.

Without a written agreement, all is not lost, but the path is harder. That path is a resulting trust by implication: even though nothing was written down, all the facts together show that the parents never intended a gift. That has to be built out of a chain of evidence: every dollar came from the parents, the taxes and carrying costs after the purchase were also funded by the parents, the child had no income of their own at the time, and so on. The more complete the chain, the better the chance. But any contradiction in the evidence, any gap the other side can point to, weakens it.

One more point, about witnesses. This is not about witnesses to an agreement. It is about witnesses to the matter as a whole. If someone can speak to the purpose of the money — that is, can show what the money was for when the parents gave it — that person can be asked to give evidence, and a judge will weigh it. But one caution: the evidence cannot be hearsay. The witness should be someone who participated in the earmarked funds themselves — someone who was actually involved in how the money was handled and what it was for — not someone who only heard about it from others and is repeating what they were told. When a judge looks at witness evidence, what matters is whether the witness personally took part in and saw the events themselves. Secondhand accounts do not hold up.

One last thing: whether the parents paid the whole price or only the down payment decides what is at stake. If the parents paid the full price and the trust is proven, the entire house is the parents'. If they paid only the down payment, the fight is only over the share that down payment represents.

## What to Do

The safest way for parents to fund a child's purchase is to keep the money out of the child's account altogether: pay the closing lawyer directly, or put the money in a separate account that is not touched for anything else before the purchase.

If the money has already gone into the child's own account, keep every record from now on. Original transfer slips, bank statements, all of it. Do not lose them.

If living expenses have already been spent out of the fund, do not avoid the question. Work out how to explain it first: how much was it? Was it a fee, or was it living expenses? Did the child discuss it with the parents at the time? A judge will ask, and the answer needs to be worked out before facing one in court.

If there is an agreement, find it and show it to the family's own lawyer first, so the lawyer can check it for gaps before deciding whether to show it to the other side. If there is none, do not backdate one and do not sign one with an old date.

Think about the witnesses in advance: can they actually testify? Can they describe how they witnessed the signing? If a witness cannot give a clear account, do not count on that agreement.

When the other side's lawyer asks for an exchange of documents, or for the child's tax returns for the past few years, do not treat that as harassment. In Ontario litigation, exchanging financial information is the normal process. If the argument is that the house belongs to the parents, the documents will have to be put before the court and the other side anyway.

## FAQs

### 1. Only a couple of hundred dollars was spent on fees. Does that count as spending?

A few hundred dollars in transaction fees can be explained, and a judge will not hold that against the child. But ordinary living expenses are a different matter. What the judge is looking at is whether the money was treated as the child's own.

### 2. Without a written agreement, is there no hope at all?

Not no hope, but the difficulty goes up a great deal. Without an agreement, the argument is left with a resulting trust by implication: proving from all the facts that the parents never intended a gift. That takes a chain of evidence — all the money came from the parents, the carrying costs afterwards were also funded by them, the child had no income of their own at the time. The more complete the chain, the better the chance, but the living expenses spent out of the fund will always be a point the other side attacks.

### 3. The house later became the matrimonial home. Does it have to be divided now?

Not necessarily. If the house was never put in the other spouse's name, and if the trust arrangement between the parents and the child was already in place before the marriage, Ontario case law has upheld such arrangements. The reasoning is that a person is entitled, before marriage, to protect their property by lawful means, and there was no obligation at that time to disclose the arrangement to someone who was then only a boyfriend or girlfriend. But the trust itself has to be proven first. If it cannot be proven, the fact that the house became the [matrimonial home](/en/blog/premarital-home-wedding-day-value-divorce-ontario/) pulls it back into the property to be divided.
