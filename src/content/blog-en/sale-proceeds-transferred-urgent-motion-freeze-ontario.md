Once the sale proceeds have been transferred away, an urgent motion can't freeze them. Money that has been moved into someone else's name can't be recovered. Urgency is the court's call, not yours. Whatever is still sitting in your spouse's account, the court can order preserved — as long as it's still there.

A client sold the [matrimonial home](/en/blog/matrimonial-home-title-vs-property-division-ontario/). The proceeds went into the couple's joint account — and within a short time, her spouse had transferred every dollar out. He said the money would be transferred back. It never was. She brought an urgent motion, asking the court to freeze his accounts and get the money back. The urgent motion was dismissed. She then brought a regular motion, and the court did make an order: whatever remained in his account was not to be touched. There were only a few dozen dollars left in it.

The short answer: money already transferred away can't be frozen, and it can't be recovered.

---

## 1. Urgency Is the Court's Call, Not Yours

"Urgent" turns on whether something is happening right now and can't wait. If your spouse transferred the money a year ago and you couldn't get it back on your own, going to court now for an order making him return it — courts generally won't treat that as urgent. The event is long past; there is no risk unfolding now, so where is the urgency? But if you catch your spouse in the act of moving money — transfers under way, a real risk the money is about to disappear — the court will grant an urgent motion and hear it quickly. A child taken from school and not returned is the same idea: it is happening now, so the court will act. Her motion was dismissed for an understandable reason: the money had been transferred before she even sued, and a month before suing, she had written to him trying to work it out. Having waited that long, she couldn't show the court any urgency. One more common mistake: putting an [equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/) claim into an urgent motion. Equalization is calculated and paid at the end of a case; no court pays out half in the middle. That request goes nowhere either.

---

## 2. What Is Still in the Account Can Be Ordered Preserved; What Is Gone Can't

An order on a regular motion only reaches money still sitting in the account. It can't undo transfers that have already happened. That is how you end up holding an order while the account holds a few dozen dollars — the order is real, and useless. And even while money is still in your spouse's account, getting an order that it not be touched isn't easy: you first have to show the court he actually intends to move it or spend it. Worry alone isn't enough. Canada is also different from China here: there is no pre-litigation asset preservation. In China, you can apply to have an account frozen first and litigate afterwards. Canada has no such mechanism — a freezing order can only be sought within an ongoing case, and only after you persuade the court there is a genuine risk the assets will be [dissipated](/en/blog/dissipation-of-assets/).

---

## 3. Once the Money Is in a Third Party's Account, Everything Depends on Who That Third Party Is

The reason it can't be frozen is that the money is no longer in your spouse's name — it is sitting in someone else's account. What you can do next depends on who received it. If the money went to your spouse's parents, there is still a path: you can apply to add the parents to the litigation as parties, or apply to freeze that non-party account. Parents are closely connected to your spouse, and a large transfer of sale proceeds into their account around the time of separation is something a court will examine seriously — was this really repayment of a debt, or was it moving assets out of reach? But if the third party is someone else, with no close connection to your spouse, courts generally will not freeze that person's account. He is simply someone who received money; your dispute with your spouse doesn't reach him.

So sequence matters. When you first see signs that your spouse is moving money, that is the time to apply for a court order — before the money is gone, not after. While the money is still there, an order works. Once it has been transferred, no amount of urgency helps. And for large joint assets like sale proceeds, whose account the money goes into and when it can be touched should be worked out with a lawyer and put in writing before the home is sold.

## FAQs

### 1. My spouse transferred all the sale proceeds. Does that mean the money is all his now?

No. Transferring it away doesn't make it exempt from division. The money was joint property; it existed on the separation date, so it goes into the equalization calculation, and he has to account for your half. The problem is enforcement: if nothing is left in his name, even a judgment may collect nothing.

### 2. I just found out my spouse is moving money right now, and it isn't all gone yet. What should I do?

See a lawyer immediately and apply for a court order that the money remaining in the account not be touched. Speed is everything — act while the money is still there. The order can only protect what hasn't been transferred yet; it can't protect what is already gone.

### 3. My spouse says the money repaid a debt to a friend. Who decides whether that is true?

Neither of you — the evidence decides. If you believe the debt is fabricated, add the person who received the money to the litigation and make him explain the debt in court. A debt with no [loan agreement](/en/blog/spouse-sudden-debts-share-ontario/), no transfer records, and no account of how the money was borrowed is one a court won't readily accept.
