A common question in a first meeting is: we are still together — can we sign a contract now that says the children stay with me, and the other parent only has visits?

In Ontario the answer is short: **you cannot lock that in.**

A prenuptial agreement and a contract signed during the marriage are the same legal instrument: a marriage contract. Property and spousal support can be negotiated. Who the children live with, and who decides, cannot. Drafting and review sit on the [domestic contracts](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/) page. This article is only about children.

---

## Can a marriage contract decide who the children live with?

Not in the way people mean.

What is still called "custody" in everyday speech splits in Ontario into [decision-making responsibility and parenting time](/en/blog/child-custody-ontario-chinese/). One is who makes the major calls on school, health and religion. The other is where the child lives and how time is shared.

A court decides both on the **best interests of the child**, not on what the parents signed years earlier. A clause written while the marriage is intact that says "the children stay with me" is one the court may ignore.

A cohabitation agreement cannot lock it either. Parenting can be written into a [separation agreement](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/#separation) after you have already separated — a different statutory list — and even then a court may disregard the terms in the child's best interests.

---

## Can a prenup decide who the children live with?

No. It is the same legal document as a during-marriage contract.

Search queries often split “prenup” from “marriage contract.” In Ontario they are not two regimes: signed before the wedding or after, both are marriage contracts under s. 52. Markham, Toronto and the rest of the province apply the same sections.

So “lock the children in before we marry” fails for the same reason as a clause signed during the marriage. Property and spousal support can still be negotiated — see [prenuptial agreements in Ontario](/en/blog/prenuptial-agreement-ontario-2026/).

## What the statute lets you write, and what it does not

The list is in s. 52(1) of the Ontario [Family Law Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90f03). Two people who are married or intend to marry may agree in writing on:

- ownership or division of property
- support obligations
- the right to direct the **education** and **moral training** of their children
- any other matter in the settlement of their affairs

The same clause is explicit: **not** the right to custody of or access to their children. The Act has not been rewritten in 2021 *Divorce Act* language. In practice that is decision-making responsibility and parenting time.

So you can write about school and how the children are raised. You cannot write a binding clause that the children live with one parent and see the other every second weekend.

Two further sections are easy to mix up:

- **s. 56(1):** on education, moral training, custody or access, the court may disregard a domestic-contract term where that is in the child's best interests.
- **s. 56(1.1):** on **child support**, the court may disregard a term that is unreasonable having regard to the Federal Child Support Guidelines and to any other child-support term in the contract.

Section 58 is about **contracts made outside Ontario** — which law governs them. Paragraph 58(c) adds that a custody or access clause in a foreign marriage contract or cohabitation agreement is not enforceable in Ontario. For a contract signed here, the operative limits are s. 52(1)(c) and s. 56(1), not s. 58 as the lead cite.

The matrimonial home has its own rule in s. 52(2): a clause that purports to limit Part II rights is unenforceable. This article does not develop that point, but a marriage contract is constrained on more than parenting.

## What happens if the contract already says "the children stay with me"?

The clause does not bind.

On separation or divorce the other parent can ask the court to decide decision-making and parenting time afresh. The court will not treat "I already gave that up" as the end of the inquiry. A parenting concession you thought you had bought may not be there when it matters.

Property is different. If disclosure was made, the signing was voluntary and the words are clear, a court will often enforce the property terms. Parenting was never something a marriage contract could lock.

---

## Can child support be the bargaining chip?

No.

A familiar trade is: take less of the property and I will not ask for child support; or transfer the house and we write support off. Between adults that looks like a deal. To the court, child support is the **child's right**, not a debt the spouses can set off.

Section 52(1)(b) lets a contract deal with support obligations, so child support can appear in the document. That is not permission to write "neither of us pays" and expect the court to follow it. An unreasonable child-support term is a s. 56(1.1) problem.

After separation, leaving support out of a separation agreement, or writing a waiver, is a separate article: [Can you leave child support out of a separation agreement?](/en/blog/child-support-waiver-separation-agreement-ontario/)

## Can a property clause leave you worse off than the statute?

Yes. Parenting will not lock. Property often will.

With no contract, the default is [equalization of net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/): the growth in each spouse's net worth during the marriage is evened out. It is not a 60/40 split of each house or account. People sometimes write themselves down to 40 percent "for the children." The children's clause may be ignored. The property clause may be enforced as signed.

Before you sign, ask what the statute already gives you. Giving up that default has to be a calculated choice, not a comfort trade against parenting language that will not hold.

## We signed after an affair. Is the contract still good?

Not automatically void. It can still be set aside.

Section 56(4) lets a court set aside a domestic contract or a provision in it, including where:

- a party failed to disclose significant assets, debts or other liabilities existing when the contract was made
- a party did not understand the nature or consequences of the contract
- otherwise in accordance with the law of contract — duress, undue influence, misrepresentation

The Ontario Court of Appeal in [LeVan v. LeVan, 2008 ONCA 388](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2008/2008onca388/2008onca388.html) uses a two-stage analysis: first, whether a s. 56(4) ground is made out; second, whether the court should exercise its discretion to set the contract aside. Meeting a ground is not an automatic result. [Ward v. Ward, 2011 ONCA 178](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2011/2011onca178/2011onca178.html) confirms that s. 56(4)(c) can pick up ordinary contract-law grounds.

A contract signed the night an affair came out is examined for whether there was a real choice, time to speak to a lawyer, and open financial figures. Those are process facts. Infidelity itself is not a separate statutory button.

The Supreme Court of Canada in [Rick v. Brandsema, 2009 SCC 10](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2009/2009scc10/2009scc10.html) dealt with a **separation** negotiation after breakdown, and with one spouse using an information imbalance. The facts are not a during-marriage signing. The principle still travels: a domestic contract is not an ordinary commercial bargain, and the process has to bear scrutiny.

Independent legal advice is, in practice, the usual safeguard: each party has their own lawyer explain what they are giving up. Ontario law does **not** make ILA a statutory condition of validity. Section 56(4)(b) asks whether the party understood the nature and consequences. Without ILA that is harder to show. It is not accurate to call ILA mandatory in the statute.

When a contract will be set aside, and how disclosure and form are tested, is in [Is your marriage contract valid?](/en/blog/prenuptial-agreement-validity-ontario/)

## Common questions

### Can a marriage contract decide who gets the kids in Ontario?

It cannot lock that in. Section 52(1)(c) allows education and moral training, not custody or access — now usually called decision-making responsibility and parenting time. The court may still decide in the child's best interests.

### Can a prenup decide custody in Ontario?

No. A prenup and a during-marriage contract are both marriage contracts under s. 52. Signing before the wedding does not lock who the children live with or who makes the decisions.

### Is a prenup different from a during-marriage contract on parenting?

No. Both are marriage contracts under s. 52. A cohabitation agreement has the same limit. A separation agreement may deal with custody and access; s. 56(1) still lets the court disregard those terms.

### If we write that neither parent pays child support, does that bind the court?

Usually not. Child support is the child's right. Section 56(1.1) lets the court disregard a term that is unreasonable having regard to the Guidelines.

### We signed after an affair came out. Can that contract be set aside?

Possibly, but not automatically. The court looks at disclosure, understanding, duress or undue influence. Infidelity is not a stand-alone ground. ILA reduces risk; it is not a statutory signing requirement.

### If the property split is worse than equalization, are we stuck?

You may be. The default is equalization of net family property, not a 60/40 split of each asset. Parenting clauses do not lock; property clauses often do.
