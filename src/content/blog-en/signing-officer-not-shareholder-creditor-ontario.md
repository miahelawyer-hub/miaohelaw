Generally speaking, no. You signed on behalf of the company, not for yourself. The company is the borrower. You are its employee, not a shareholder. The creditor can go after the company, its shareholders, and its directors, but not the person who signed the paperwork. That said, you need to check what you actually signed.

She works for her husband's company, where she signs the cheques, the contracts, and the loan papers. She owns no shares in the company. Now they are separating, and she is worried the bank will come after her because her signature is on the loan documents.

She signed for the company, not for herself, so the creditor should not come after her.

---

## 1. A signing officer is an employee, not a shareholder

A company authorizes certain people to sign its cheques, contracts, and loan documents. When you sign, you are signing for the company, not for yourself. The company is the borrower. You are only its agent, acting on its instructions. Signing a document does not make you an [owner](/en/blog/company-in-my-name-never-looked-books-divorce-ontario/), and it does not make you a borrower.

---

## 2. The creditor looks at the shareholders and directors, not at whoever signed the paper

To find out who stands behind a company, a creditor checks the corporate records, the annual return and the tax filings. Those records name the shareholders and the directors. Your name on a signature line is not in them. You are not an owner, so the creditor's claim is not aimed at you.

---

## 3. That answer depends on what you actually signed

Two things would change the answer. The first is a [personal guarantee](/en/blog/spouse-company-loan-no-personal-guarantee-ontario/). If you signed one, you promised to pay if the company does not, and the creditor can come after you. The second is how you signed. If the signature block shows you signed for and on behalf of the company, you are protected. If it shows only your name, with no mention of the company, the creditor may argue you signed for yourself. Read the loan documents. What matters is what is on the paper.

---

## 4. If the creditor does come after you, show that you signed as an agent

The corporate records are your evidence. The annual return and the tax filings list the shareholders and the directors, and your name is not among them. The loan documents name the company as the borrower. Take those papers to your lawyer. A letter from your lawyer, with the corporate records attached, tells the creditor it has the wrong person.

## Frequently asked questions

### 1. Does signing the company's cheques and contracts matter?

No. Day-to-day paperwork works the same way. You signed as the company's agent, not in your personal capacity. The company is responsible for those documents, not you.

### 2. What if I am a director?

A director is different from a signing officer. Directors can be personally liable for certain things, such as unpaid wages and certain tax remittances. But a director is not automatically liable for the company's bank loan. That depends on whether the director signed a personal guarantee.

### 3. What if the creditor has already sued me?

Do not ignore it. A claim has deadlines, and if you miss one, the court can enter a [default judgment](/en/blog/default-judgment-divorce-ontario/) against you. Take the loan documents and the corporate records to a lawyer right away. If you signed only as an agent, your lawyer can ask the court to remove you from the claim.
