The government may no longer hold your parents' original immigration records.

Most people assume archives are kept permanently — which is why paperwork feels like something that can always be dealt with later. When you actually need a record from several decades ago, the answer may simply be: no record found.

This is not hypothetical. It comes up repeatedly when Chinese families apply for [Ontario probate](/en/estate-trustee/) or for an inheritance notarial certificate in China — and usually only after the person has died and the process has already started.

The living-person declaration has its own [service page](/en/hague-apostille/same-name-declaration/). This article draws the line: after death it cannot be done. So if you have changed a name, renewed a passport, or ever used a former name — or if spelling, spaces or name order differ, whether or not you still use that version — complete a same-person declaration while you can. It is a precaution, not something to leave for later.

---

## Can you still make a same-person declaration after someone has died?

No.

While they are alive, a same-person declaration (also called a same-name declaration or a one-and-the-same-person statement) requires the person to **attend in person**: produce the passport and ID, swear and sign. After Ontario notarization it goes to ODS for an [Apostille](/en/hague-apostille/) and is used at a housing bureau, notary office or bank in China. It can be done in one step.

Once they have died, no one can sign and say, for them, "both of these names are me." The same-person declaration is no longer available. All that remains is **to gather the old documents and government records that still exist.** If those connect the two names, an Ontario estate or a China inheritance may continue. If they do not, you cannot prove it.

The conclusion is not that paperwork can wait. It is that any historical difference in how a name was written should be documented while the person can still sign.

---

## Who should make a same-person declaration now?

Do not wait for an estate to open, or for an office to send the file back. If any of the following is true, do a [same-person declaration](/en/hague-apostille/same-name-declaration/) while the person is living, as a precaution:

1. **They have changed a name** — a married surname, a formal change of name, a Chinese name replaced by an English one, or the reverse.
2. **They have renewed a passport** — pinyin, spaces and middle names on the old and new passports are often already different.
3. **They have a former name (曾用名)** — still on a hukou, Chinese ID or property deed, even if nobody uses it day to day.

These also count as "not the same name," whichever version is used now:

- different romanisation (Xiao / Hsiao, Wei / Way)
- a space, no space, or a hyphen (Li Ming / LiMing / Li-Ming)
- surname and given name in reverse order (Zhang Wei / Wei Zhang)
- traditional versus simplified characters, or a middle name that appears on some documents and not others
- a mismatch across an old passport, old ID, PR card and citizenship card

**It does not matter whether that version is still in use.** A notary office, housing bureau or bank in China checks the characters on the papers, not the name you go by. While the person is living, one declaration lists every historical spelling. After death, "we all know it is the same person" cannot replace a same-person declaration.

## Three layers at once is what freezes an estate

In immigrant families it is very common for an older relative to have used different names at different stages of life. The name changes, passport renewals, former names, spelling and spacing listed above are all the same problem.

The difficulty arises when three things happen at once:

1. an older relative used different names on different documents, and this has to be reconciled;
2. that relative has died and can no longer swear a declaration;
3. the generation above them also left an estate unsettled — and sometimes no death registration can be located.

Each layer on its own is routine. Stacked together, every step waits on the one before it: the earlier estate cannot proceed until the heir's identity is established; that identity depends on old documents or government records connecting the two names; and that person is no longer alive to make a same-person declaration.

The estate then sits still — sometimes for years.

When [heirs live in China](/en/estate-trustee/china-heirs/), the Ontario certificate and the China notarial file have to sit on one timetable. A name mismatch stops both.

---

## Why it becomes a different order of difficulty after death

Because a same-person declaration can no longer be made. You are left with whatever other people still hold:

- expired passports, old ID cards and marriage certificates that the family has not thrown out
- government archives — which have retention periods, and are destroyed once those expire
- records that may not have migrated intact after an institution merged or a system was replaced

People who remember the history grow old and pass away. After death, their recollection cannot stand in for a same-person declaration either. Only documents and records count.

The same logic applies to the generation above. If no death registration can be found, and no other old document or government record ties the names together, you cannot prove it. It is not a missing form to be replaced.

Start looking for the old passport before the bank or the notary office sends the file back.

---

## The government says "no record." Are there other archives?

One department reporting "no record" does not mean the government holds nothing. Different lines of business often keep separate archives, each requiring its own request.

On the Canadian side, the usual split is:

- **immigration files** and **citizenship / naturalization files** are not the same system. One window may be empty while the other is not.
- **Ontario vital statistics** (birth, marriage, death) are held provincially, separately from federal immigration.
- older naturalization material is sometimes already at Library and Archives Canada, not at today's immigration counter.
- a formal change of name may still sit as a court order in Ontario.

Which system still has a file — and which request to use — depends on the year of arrival, citizenship and any name change, and on the province. Do not stop after one "no record." If every system has been searched, and the old ID at home has been searched, and nothing connects the names, you cannot prove it.

---

## How do records from China, and Apostille, connect to Ontario?

Birth registrations, hukou, ID issuance and historical passport files in the country of origin are held independently of Canadian archives. In Chinese families, the missing link is often on that side.

Do not reverse the direction:

- **Ontario documents used in China** (a same-person declaration made while the person was living, or other Ontario-notarized or court documents going to a China notary or land registry): follow the [Apostille](/en/hague-apostille/) path. Ontario-issued or Ontario-notarized papers go to ODS.
- **China documents used in Ontario** (a notarial certificate of kinship or identity): the [Apostille hub](/en/hague-apostille/) explains that a certificate from a China notary office has an accepted path in Ontario proceedings; documents already in Canada can also be certified as true copies or supported by a statutory declaration before an Ontario notary. Dual licensing is what lets each document take the right path.

A country that is not in the Apostille Convention may still need consular legalisation. Follow the receiving office's current rules. Do not assume China and Canada are the template for everywhere else.

China inheritance often also needs a [parent-child declaration](/en/hague-apostille/parent-child-declaration/) or a [waiver of succession](/en/hague-apostille/renunciation-of-inheritance/). If the names are still in conflict, those documents stall too.

---

## How do old documents connect the two names?

After death, this is all that is left. What you want is one document showing both names at once, or two government records or old IDs linked by the same date of birth, the same address, or the same marriage.

Typical sources:

- marriage certificates (which usually record the pre-marriage name)
- expired or cancelled passports and old identity cards
- driver's licence history
- employment and banking records
- land registration and mortgage documents
- historical tax records
- hukou, ID issuance and historical passport files in China

**Do not discard expired documents.** They are often the only physical link between an old name and a current one. One document showing both names is worth ten documents showing only one.

---

## If the old documents and government records are gone, can you still prove it?

No.

After death you cannot make a same-person declaration. All that remains is old ID and government records that still exist. If those are gone, you cannot prove the two names belong to the same person. An [Ontario probate](/en/estate-trustee/) application or a China inheritance will stop there.

There is no further procedure that fills the gap. That is why the same-person declaration has to be done while the person is still living.

---

## What should you do while everyone is still living?

After death, whether you can prove the names depends entirely on whether the old documents and government records still exist. Prevention costs almost nothing by comparison.

**If they have changed a name, renewed a passport, or ever used a former name, do the [same-person declaration](/en/hague-apostille/same-name-declaration/) now.** Do the same if spelling, spaces or name order differ — whether or not that version is still in daily use. Do not leave the next generation to search archives that may already be gone.

**Keep a copy of every document ever used.** Scan them even if they are expired, cancelled or replaced: passports from every country, including cancelled ones; identity documents; birth certificates; marriage certificates; immigration and citizenship papers; driver's licences. When a new passport is issued, keep the old one.

**Do not leave the previous generation's estate unsettled.** If your parents' generation left an estate unresolved, deal with it while they are still living. Once two generations have passed, a missing record means you cannot prove it.

## Common questions

### Can you still make a same-person declaration after someone has died?

No. A same-person declaration requires the person to attend, produce ID, swear and sign. After death that route is gone. All that remains is old documents and government records that still exist. For a living person, see the [same-name declaration](/en/hague-apostille/same-name-declaration/).

### Are "same-person" and "same-name" declarations the same thing?

On this site and at receiving offices in China, both names refer to the same document: proof that the English name on a passport, the name on a Chinese ID, and the name on a hukou or property deed belong to one person. While the person is living, it is notarized in Ontario and apostilled for use in China. After death it cannot be done.

### The government says there is no naturalization record. Is the estate finished?

One office reporting "no record" does not mean every government system is empty. Immigration files, citizenship files and provincial vital statistics are often stored separately, and old passports or a marriage certificate at home still count. But if the old documents and the government records are all gone, you cannot prove the two names belong to the same person.

### The estate is stuck because the names do not match. Can it still proceed?

Only if you can still find old documents or government records that connect the two names. If you can, an Ontario estate or a China inheritance may continue. If you cannot, you cannot prove it, and the file stops.

### China needs same-person proof and the person has already died. What then?

After death you cannot make a same-person declaration. A China notary office or land registry needs old ID and records that match the names. If those do not exist, you cannot prove it is the same person.

### What should we keep while older relatives are still living?

If they have changed a name, renewed a passport, or ever used a former name — or if spelling, spaces or name order differ — do the same-person declaration now, whether or not that version is still in daily use. Keep expired passports. Finish the previous generation's estate while they are still here.

### We changed a name, renewed a passport, or have a former name we no longer use. Do we still need a same-person declaration?

Yes. Receiving offices check the spellings that appeared on documents, not the name you use day to day. An old passport, a former name, a missing space, or a reversed surname and given name can stall inheritance, a property transfer or a bank. While the person is living, one declaration connects those versions. After death it cannot be done.
