No. If you did not remarry, you are [cohabiting](/en/family-law/common-law/), not married. Having been married to each other before does not mean you are still spouses now. After a divorce, if the same two people move back in together but do not legally remarry, Ontario property law does not apply the married spouses' [equalization](/en/family-law/property-division/) system. For cohabiting partners, title comes first: jointly owned property is divided according to title; if your name is on the house, you can claim your half; if your name is not on the house, you do not automatically receive half.

A client came to consult me. It was the same two people. They had been married before and later divorced. After the divorce, they moved back in together, but they never remarried or obtained a new marriage certificate. Now that relationship has also come to an end, and she asked whether the house could still be divided as if they were married spouses.

The short answer: no. Without remarriage, there is no married-property equalization.

---

## 1. The Divorce Ended the Earlier Marriage. Later Cohabitation Does Not Automatically Reconnect It

The law does not ask whether, emotionally, these are still the same two people, or whether they were married at some earlier point. The question is whether a marriage exists now. Once they divorced, the marriage ended. Moving back in together without legally remarrying does not create a new marriage. Property from the first marriage should have been dealt with at the time of that divorce, by agreement or through the court process. A later period of cohabitation does not revive the property rules of the earlier marriage.

---

## 2. Ontario Cohabitation Has No Property Equalization

The married-property calculation, often described as splitting family property through equalization, is for married spouses. Ontario common-law partners do not have that system. The same is true for these two people: they divorced earlier and later lived together again, so the relationship after the divorce is a common-law relationship, dealt with as cohabitation rather than as a marriage. Living together for three years, five years, or ten years does not automatically turn the property into a 50/50 split. The idea that property is divided after a few years of cohabitation comes from British Columbia, not Ontario. When an Ontario cohabiting relationship ends, property generally remains with the person in whose name it is held. Only jointly owned property is divided, and it is divided according to title.

---

## 3. For the House, the Key Question Is Whose Name Is on Title

If the house is in both names, and your name is on title, you can claim your half based on ownership. For jointly titled property, the starting point is the registered ownership, not a fresh accounting of who paid more and who paid less.

If the house is in his name alone, the position is different. Having once been married to him, and later living with him again, does not by itself give you half of that house. To make a claim, you would have to proceed on a different basis: proving a property-related contribution by you, such as paying part of the down payment, paying the mortgage, paying for renovations, or showing that the two of you in fact acquired or built up the property together. Those claims depend on evidence. They are not an automatic equal split, and they are not established by the number of years you lived together alone.

---

## 4. If You Want Married-Property Rules to Apply, There Must Be a Marriage — or the Cohabitation Arrangement Should Be Written Down

If the two people later actually [remarry](/en/blog/divorced-china-remarried-property-from-which-date-ontario/), a new marriage begins, and property issues are dealt with under the rules for that marriage. If they do not remarry and simply continue living together, but want the house, accounts, and contributions dealt with clearly, they should sign a [cohabitation agreement](/en/blog/prenuptial-agreement-ontario-2026/) setting out what belongs to each person, how jointly owned property is to be divided, and how contributions during the cohabitation are to be treated. Without remarriage and without an agreement, a dispute comes back to title and evidence.

## FAQs

### 1. We are the same two people. We divorced before and then lived together again for years. Doesn't that make us spouses again?

Emotionally, it is the same two people. Legally, no. The divorce ended the marriage. If you later lived together without remarrying, that is cohabitation. The length of the cohabitation does not turn it back into a marriage. The house is not equalized as married property; title is the starting point.

### 2. After three years together, aren't we common-law partners, so the house must be split?

Do not mix up [support](/en/family-law/support/) and property. A longer cohabitation may matter for spousal support eligibility, but property equalization applies to married spouses. Being common-law partners in Ontario does not make a house automatically half and half.

### 3. The house is only in his name, but I also put money into it while we lived together. Can I get that back?

Possibly, but not through married-property equalization. You would need evidence of your contributions and a cohabitation property claim, such as unjust enrichment or a [constructive trust](/en/blog/in-laws-name-house-lived-decade-divorce-ontario/) claim. What can be recovered depends on what the evidence proves. It is not calculated as an automatic one-half share.
