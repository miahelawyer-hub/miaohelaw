No. Equalization and spousal support belong to the spouse, not the children. You can't have the money paid into the children's names, you can't restrict how your ex spends it, and you can't force them to put it in a trust for the kids. Once it's paid, it's theirs.

---

## 1. This Is a Bill Between Two Spouses — the Children Aren't Part of It

Equalization and spousal support are accounts settled between spouses. The equalization payment is the result of equalizing the spouses' [net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/): each side's net property on separation day is calculated, and the one with more pays half the difference — to the spouse. Spousal support is paid to the spouse personally, tied to her own needs and what she contributed to the family.

Once it's in her hands, how she spends it is her business. People say: she'll waste it, it'll be gone in no time. That worry is not your concern — the law has never said that if she spends it all, you have to pay more.

The children have their own separate entitlement: [child support](/en/blog/child-support-waiver-separation-agreement-ontario/). That's the children's money, calculated by the tables, paid separately.

---

## 2. Once Paid, It's Theirs — You Don't Control How It's Spent

An equalization payment is a debt you owe your ex. Once paid, it's her property. She can buy a house, save it, or spend it — none of it is your concern.

Some payers aren't content to just pay. They want conditions: the money must be spent on the children, it mustn't be wasted. The law has a name for this — dead-hand control: the property has already been divided and transferred, yet the person who used to own it still wants to control how it's used. The term comes from wills and trusts, where someone long gone tries to govern how the living spend money through conditions in a will. The law has always limited that kind of control — conditions that offend public policy are simply void.

The reasoning carries over: once property has been distributed and transferred, control ends. An equalization payment is no different. The moment it's paid, it's your ex's. Attaching conditions is trying to keep a grip on money that is no longer yours, with a hand that has already let go.

Spousal support is the same. It's money for the spouse personally. You can't demand proof it was spent on the children, and you can't dictate what it's used for.

A court order will never say "this money must be spent on the children" — because that isn't what this money is.

---

## 3. You Can't Force a Trust for the Children Either

"I don't trust her — she'll spend it all" is an understandable worry. Legally, it goes nowhere.

You can't control your ex, and you can't control how she spends her money. You have no such right, and the law won't support you. Once the property is divided, it's her money. You're both adults, and every adult has the right to manage their own property. Even if she spends every cent the very next day, that's her freedom.

So you can't ask the court to order your ex to put her equalization money into a trust for the children. A trust can only dispose of your own money, never someone else's.

---

## What to Do

To leave money for the children, use your own: set up your own trust, name the children as [beneficiaries on your insurance](/en/blog/separation-agreement-waiver-still-need-will-ontario/), arrange it in your [will](/en/blog/will-without-divorce-kids-cannot-get-half-ontario/). That money reaches the children directly, never through your ex.

In negotiations you can propose that both of you save for the children or set up trusts — but that's by agreement. It requires her consent; it isn't your right.

Don't litigate on "she'll waste the money": it has no legal footing, and it burns legal fees.

## FAQs

### 1. What about child support — can my ex just spend that however she likes too?

Child support is different in nature: it's the children's money, calculated under the government tables, and parents can't privately agree to reduce it. But it's still paid to the parent who mainly cares for the children, and you can't demand an itemized accounting of it.

### 2. She'll waste the money and the kids will get nothing. Is there really nothing I can do?

About her share — no. It's her money. What you can do is arrange your own share properly: trusts, insurance beneficiaries, a will, all set up in advance, so your money goes straight to the children without passing through her hands.

### 3. Can a separation agreement require her to put the money in a trust for the kids?

If both sides agree, yes — but it can't be just one sentence in the agreement. The trust documents must be completed and signed at the same time: who the trustee is, how the money is managed, when the children can access it — all settled in the trust documents themselves. A bare line saying "she agrees to set up a trust," with no actual trust in place, is an empty promise; there's nothing to enforce later. If she doesn't agree, a court won't order it on your say-so. Holding up the whole agreement over this condition only hurts you in the end.
