The value, yes. A [prenup](/en/blog/already-married-marriage-contract-home-down-payment-ontario/) can agree that the house's value belongs to one spouse, even after you move in. But possession is a different matter. Once you live in it as a couple, it is a [matrimonial home](/en/blog/house-in-his-name-designate-matrimonial-home-ontario/), and selling it may need your spouse's signature.

A client came to consult me. Before the wedding, she and her fiancé signed a prenuptial agreement listing her condo as excluded property. Its value, including any future appreciation, would stay hers. After the marriage, they moved into the condo and lived there. She came to ask: the agreement says the condo is mine, but now we live in it. Does the exclusion still hold?

The short answer: the value stays protected, but the right of possession cannot be limited by agreement.

---

## 1. The Value Can Be Agreed to Belong to One Spouse

A prenuptial agreement can say that a house, including its future appreciation, stays one spouse's separate property and does not go into [equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/). That agreement stands even after you move in. If the house is sold, the money goes to the spouse the agreement names.

However, the tricky part is how the agreement is worded. After reviewing the case law, you will see that a judge examines the certainty of the wording. Some people sign an agreement using vague language such as "all of the property under my name is mine." That may be held invalid or unenforceable when a dispute arises. It is unclear. All of the property? Which one are you referring to? That is too broad, and assets change during the relationship. In that situation, the judge may exercise discretion to set aside the agreement. In the case law, the agreements that are recognized are the ones that specify the property, and specify that the agreement covers not just ownership, but the entire value or the equity. You need to make it very clear, and the parties need to state clearly that even if they move in and the property becomes a matrimonial home, they still want the same division. The clearer the agreement, the more likely it is to be enforced.

---

## 2. The Right of Possession Cannot Be Limited by Agreement

Under Ontario law, a prenup or a postnup cannot limit a spouse's right to live in the matrimonial home. You can agree on who owns the value, but you cannot agree to keep a spouse out of the home, or to decide when one of you must move out. The agreement's power stops at the value.

That is because the right of possession does not come from the agreement. It comes from the matrimonial home rules in Part II of the Family Law Act. Both spouses have an equal [right to live](/en/blog/house-in-spouse-name-stay-with-kids-separation-ontario/) in the matrimonial home, even if the house is in only one spouse's name. The owner cannot change the locks, cannot throw the other spouse out, and cannot use the agreement to do it for them. A clause that tries to do that will not hold.

The right does not end at separation either. Spouses who have separated can still both live in the home. They count as [separated under the same roof](/en/blog/same-roof-wedding-separation-date-ontario/) if they do not share a room and keep their finances separate. Wanting the house does not entitle one spouse to force the other out. Both stay until the home is sold or a court orders otherwise, and if one refuses to leave after a sale, the other can ask the court for an eviction order.

The only fast way one spouse leaves is safety. If there are threats or violence, the police can remove the aggressor and help obtain a [restraining order](/en/blog/ontario-restraining-order-chinese-family/). That is about safety, not about the agreement, and not about who owns the house.

If one spouse wants exclusive possession of the home, the route is a court order, not the agreement. The court decides on the facts, not on what the agreement says. It looks at the children's best interests, each spouse's circumstances, and safety. The agreement controls the money. The statute controls who can live there. The two run on separate tracks, and one does not override the other.

---

## 3. Moving In Turns It into a Matrimonial Home

A matrimonial home is the home the spouses ordinarily live in as their family residence. Once you both live there, the house is a matrimonial home, whatever the agreement says about its value. And a matrimonial home cannot be sold or mortgaged without the other spouse's consent. So selling it may need your spouse's signature.

Title does not decide this. A house in only one spouse's name can still be a matrimonial home. What matters is whether the spouses live in it as their family residence, not whose name is on the deed. And the status follows where you live: when you move to a new primary residence, the new home becomes the matrimonial home.

But there is a tricky part here. Whether a property is a matrimonial home is determined at the date of separation. The home you are living in then is the matrimonial home. So even if you moved in and lived there, if after several years you move out or change your primary residence, the property we are talking about, the one excluded from the net family property, is no longer a matrimonial home. The matrimonial home is a dynamic thing. Living in a property once does not make it a matrimonial home forever.

## FAQs

### 1. We signed the prenup years ago. Do we need to update it?

If there has been a material change, yes. If the excluded property became the matrimonial home, or the assets have changed so much that they can no longer be traced back to the agreement, the old agreement may no longer protect you. The best protection then is a new agreement that deals with the new situation.

### 2. The agreement says my spouse gives up the right to live in the house. Does that work?

It depends on what the property is. If the property is a matrimonial home, no. Ontario law does not allow an agreement to limit the right of possession of a matrimonial home, so that clause will not hold. But if the property is an investment property, not a matrimonial home, then you are free to agree as you wish.

### 3. What if my spouse refuses to sign? Can the house never be sold?

Selling a matrimonial home needs your spouse's consent. If your spouse refuses, you can ask the court to deal with it, but that means a court application and time.
