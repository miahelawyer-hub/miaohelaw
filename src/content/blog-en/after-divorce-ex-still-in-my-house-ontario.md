This comes up all the time. You're divorced, the house is in your name, but your ex is still living there. You have to sort it into three situations.

## 1. You agreed they could stay

If you said yes, the agreement has to be clear: is there rent or not.

If there's rent, they're basically your tenant. How much, what day each month, which account, what happens if they're late, how long they can stay, who pays property tax, insurance, maintenance — write it all down.

Unpaid rent becomes a debt. You can sue for it. With a written agreement, enforcement is easy.

The worst case is a verbal "just stay for now." How long is "for now," is there rent, no one knows. That's a fight waiting to happen.

---

## 2. Living for free, but with a court order

You have to ask why they're living for free.

If the divorce judgment says they have a real need and gives them a lifetime right to stay, and you can't kick them out — that's court permission.

In that case you can't remove them. Don't change the locks, don't cut utilities. It will hurt you.

A lot of people don't get it: "The house is in my name, why can't I make them leave?" Simple: ownership is yours, the right to occupy was given to them by the court. Two different things.

---

## 3. Just refusing to leave

No rent, no court order, just won't go.

You can sue for payment or ask the court to order them out. But the burden isn't low.

You have to prove the house is yours, they have no lawful reason to stay, you gave reasonable notice, and how their staying affects you. "I just don't want them here" isn't enough.

A lot of people in this spot choose to [sell](/en/blog/force-sale-matrimonial-home-ontario/). You can apply to the court in the sale process — when the house is sold, they have to leave. If they won't vacate, you can seek eviction.

Selling isn't one sentence, it's a process. The court looks at who's living there, whether kids are involved, how proceeds are handled. But compared to dragging it out, it's cleaner.

## At the end of the day, it's an unfinished divorce

These headaches almost always come from a divorce that wasn't fully wrapped up.

When we handle a divorce, we don't just do the divorce. We flag the risks after divorce and give comprehensive advice.

Even with an agreed [separation agreement](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/), we spell out the terms: how money is paid, deadlines, what happens if they don't pay, how the matrimonial home is handled, what the living arrangement is. Clear terms mean you can actually enforce it later.

If you go through court, don't just walk away with a divorce certificate. Think ahead about what orders you need to protect yourself down the road. Certificate in hand, house not sorted, move-out never addressed — that's future litigation.

## If the problem is already there, what now

If it wasn't handled and you're stuck now, look at what can still be fixed.

Have them pay rent and put a new agreement in writing with rent, term, and move-out timing. Or apply to [sell](/en/blog/force-sale-matrimonial-home-ontario/) and go through the court for vacant possession. Different facts, different play.

Don't delay. The longer they stay and you say nothing, the more the court may think you acquiesced, and the harder it gets.
