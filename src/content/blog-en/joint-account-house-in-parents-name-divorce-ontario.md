Yes, it counts. Whose name is on the title is not the same as who owns the value. The purchase money was paid to the lawyer from the two of your joint account, and there is no gift letter — in law, that is a [resulting trust](/en/blog/resulting-trust-parental-gifts-ontario/): the parents hold the house in name only, holding it for the two of you. When the divorce accounting is done, this house still goes into the calculation as property belonging to the two of you.

A client came to consult me. When the house was bought, it was put in her husband's parents' names, but the purchase money was paid directly to the lawyer from the couple's joint account. Now that they are divorcing, he says: the house is in my parents' names; it has nothing to do with you. Her question: does this house really not count at all?

The short answer: it counts. Registration in the parents' names does not keep it out.

---

## 1. Divorce Accounting Looks at Beneficial Ownership, Not the Name on Title

Ontario's [property division](/en/family-law/property-division/) on divorce counts who actually owns the value of the property, not whose name appears on the deed. Registration in the parents' names is a matter of form; who paid the money and who holds the real interest is what the court looks at. "It's in my parents' names" alone cannot take a house off the books.

---

## 2. Money Paid from a Joint Account, with No Gift Letter, Is a Resulting Trust

The purchase money went straight from the two of your joint account to the lawyer — a clear chain of funding. The law's starting point is that the person who paid is the real beneficiary, and the person named on title merely holds it for the payer — that is a resulting trust. For the other side to say this money was a gift to the parents, they have to produce evidence, and the most direct evidence is a [gift letter](/en/blog/chinese-divorce-property-gift-loan-trust-ontario/). Without a gift letter, the presumption stands.

---

## 3. Bringing the House Back into the Accounting Only Requires Proving the Money Came from Your Account

Bank statements, the lawyer's receipts, the closing documents — produce them, show that the purchase money came out of the joint account, and that is enough. Once the funding is proved, the house goes into the calculation as property of the two of you, as part of the [equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/). You do not need to sue first to transfer the house back before the accounting — the accounting and the transfer are two different things.

---

## 4. For the Other Side to Take the House Out of the Accounting, They Have to Prove It Was a Gift

If the other side, or the parents, want to say the money was given to the parents and the house is theirs, they need evidence of a gift: a gift letter, a written agreement from the time, something that shows an intention to give. "It's in my parents' names, so it's my parents'" is not enough on its own. Without evidence, the resulting trust cannot be displaced.

---

## FAQs

### 1. The house is in the parents' names. In the divorce, can we just sell it and split the money?

The accounting and the disposition are two different things. The house going into the equalization calculation means its value counts as part of your property. But selling the house or transferring it still involves the parents' title, and that has to be dealt with separately, depending on the circumstances. In most cases, the house stays on the books and is offset in money — the house itself does not have to be touched.

### 2. The parents say the money was theirs all along, and our account was just used to pass it through. What then?

Then it comes down to evidence. Where the money came from, whose hands it was in before it entered the joint account, whether the parents have their own records of funding it — the judge looks at the complete chain of funding, not at either side's say-so. Money in a joint account is ordinarily presumed to belong to the two of them.

### 3. If a gift letter was signed when the house was bought, does that really take it out?

With a gift letter, the other side has evidence, and the picture changes. But a gift letter is not the last word by itself — the judge will still look at all the circumstances at the time. Without a gift letter, though, the resulting trust side is on much firmer ground.
