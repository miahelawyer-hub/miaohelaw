No, it does not count. Let me put the conclusion first. A [divorce application](/en/blog/uncontested-divorce-ontario/) has to be served by special service, and the one person who cannot do the serving is the applicant. The documents have to be delivered by someone who is at least 18 years old and who is not a party to the case. So if your spouse turned up and shoved the application into your hands herself, that service does not comply with the Rules, and on that point you can relax. But I want to be just as clear about the other side of it: non-compliant service does not mean you can pretend the case does not exist. The documents are sitting in your hands, and the court has the power to approve service that was done the wrong way. Ignore them, and in the end you are the one who pays for it.

---

## 1. An Application Served by the Applicant Personally Does Not Count

Rule 6 of the Family Law Rules is clear on this. An application, and that includes a divorce application, must be served by special service. Special service has a number of requirements, and one of them is not negotiable: the person doing the serving cannot be the applicant. It has to be someone who is at least 18 and who is not a party to the case. That can be a friend or a family member the applicant asks, or a professional process server the applicant pays for. Unless a court has specifically ordered that the applicant may serve the documents personally, service by the applicant simply does not count.

There are two reasons for this rule, and they are both practical.

The first is proof. Once the documents have been served, the person who served them signs an Affidavit of Service, Form 6B, which sets out the day, the time, the place, and how the documents were handed over, and it gets filed with the court. Now imagine the applicant does the serving herself. Later she tells the court, I delivered them. You tell the court, no, she did not. There was nobody else there, so who is the court supposed to believe? Having a person with no stake in the case do the serving means there is a witness whose word the court can rely on.

The second reason is conflict. Two people in the middle of separating, and one of them turns up and puts a divorce application into the other's hands — that moment is tense enough on its own, and it does not take much for it to turn into a shouting match or worse. The Rules take the parties themselves out of the serving role so that a case does not start with an incident before it has even begun.

So the problem is not really how the documents were handed to you. The problem is that the person who handed them to you was never allowed to be the one doing it. When the server is the wrong person, nothing about the delivery itself can fix that.

---

## 2. What Counts as Valid Service

There are only a handful of ways to do special service properly:

A third party hands the documents to the person in person. If the person will not take them, that is fine — the server can put the documents down in front of them, on the ground or at their feet, as long as it is done in their presence. Whether you accept the documents or not has no bearing on whether service happened.

The documents can be left with the person's lawyer, but only if the lawyer accepts service in writing. A verbal okay is not enough.

The documents can be mailed, with an Acknowledgment of Service card, Form 6A, enclosed. The person signs the card and mails it back. The date on which the card is signed is the date service takes effect, and the time to respond runs from that date.

The documents can be left at the person's home: one copy, in a sealed envelope, handed to an adult who lives at that residence, and then a second copy mailed to the same address that day or the day after.

Whichever method is used, the server then signs the Form 6B affidavit and files it with the court, and only then is the service step actually complete. Once service has taken effect, where the documents were served in Ontario, the respondent has 30 days to file an Answer, counted from the effective date of service.

---

## 3. Irregular Service Is Not a Free Pass

I want to be firm about this part, because it is where people get themselves into trouble. Irregular service and "there is no case" are two completely different things. You cannot treat an application as though it does not exist just because it reached you the wrong way.

When the applicant realizes the service was done wrong, there are two ways she can deal with it. The simple way is to start over: have someone serve the documents again, properly this time, and the 30 days run from that second service. The other way is to go to court and ask for an order approving the first, irregular service. That is what Rule 6(18) of the Family Law Rules is for. It says that where a document was served by a method the Rules do not allow, the court may still approve the service, as long as the document actually came to the attention of the person it was meant for, or it would have come to their attention if they had not been avoiding it.

Now look at your own situation. The documents are in your hands. You have read what is in them. You have taken them to a lawyer and asked questions about them. Put together, all of that shows the documents did exactly what service is supposed to do — they reached you, and you know about the case. If a court approves the service on that basis, your time to file an Answer may well be counted from the day the documents actually arrived. If you have spent that time assuming the clock never started, you can find yourself out of time. From there, the applicant can ask to have you noted in [default](/en/blog/default-judgment-divorce-ontario/), and the divorce and the property issues can move ahead without your side of the story in front of the court.

So the advice I always give is this. If the service was defective, by all means say so — that argument is there to be made. But at the same time, get your Answer ready inside 30 days counted from the day the documents reached you. Hold that line first. Do not gamble with a deadline.

---

## 4. You Can Insist on Re-Service — But Be Clear About Why

Because the service was irregular, you are entitled to insist that the applicant serve you again and do it properly. That right is yours. But before you insist on it, I would ask you to think about what you are actually trying to get out of it.

Most of the time, when people fight about service, what they are really fighting about is time. Thirty days feels short, and they do not think they can get an Answer ready in it. If that is the situation, then honestly, there is no need to spend money on a motion. You can ask for an extension — you or your lawyer writes to the applicant's lawyer and asks for more time to file the Answer. In practice, that request is granted all the time. Opposing counsel will normally agree, nobody has to go to court, and you have saved yourself the cost and the time that a motion involves.

A motion becomes worth it in a different kind of situation: where the timing itself will have a decisive effect on your case. Maybe a deadline passing means a right you have is gone. Maybe there is a particular date that matters so much that a few days either way change the outcome. In a situation like that, yes, bring the motion, put the defective service in front of a judge, and have it decided properly.

So it comes back to what you are after. If all you are after is a few more days, ask for them — an extension is usually there for the asking, and there is no reason to spend money on a motion to get it. If the timing itself is going to shape how your case turns out, that is when a motion is worth bringing, and worth paying for.

## What to Do

Keep the documents, and keep the envelope they came in. Then, while it is fresh, write down what happened: the date you received them, who handed them to you, where it happened, and what was said. There are two reasons to do this. One, if you later argue the service was defective, that note is your evidence. Two, you need to know which day your time starts running from, and guessing later is not good enough.

If the documents came by mail and there is a card inside asking you to sign it and send it back, do not sign it yet. The moment you sign that card, service takes effect and your time starts running. Show it to a lawyer first and decide after you have had advice.

Take the documents to a lawyer promptly. The service problem and the Answer are two separate tracks: your lawyer can write to the other side and put your position on service in writing, while at the same time preparing your Answer to the deadline. Doing one does not get in the way of the other.

Whatever you do, do not sit on the documents because you have concluded the service "doesn't count." A defect like this is easy for the other side to fix. An order made against you in default is a great deal harder to undo.

## FAQs

### 1. A process server shoved the papers at me. I refused, and he dropped them at my feet. Does that count?

Yes, that counts, and it is proper service. The server was a professional with no involvement in the case, and he delivered the documents to you in person, which is exactly what the Rules ask for. Whether you take them out of his hand is up to you; when you refuse and he leaves them at your feet, the service is still complete. He will then describe the whole thing in his Form 6B affidavit — the time, the place, and how you reacted — in some detail.

### 2. My spouse stuffed the papers in my mailbox / under my door. Does that count?

No, and it is wrong in two separate ways. First, your spouse is not allowed to serve the application at all — that alone ends the question. Second, even if someone who was allowed to serve had done it, that is not how you serve documents at a home. The Rules require the documents to be handed to an adult who lives there, with a second copy sent by mail. Putting them in a mailbox or pushing them under a door meets neither requirement.

### 3. If the service doesn't count, do I even need to respond?

I would not think about it that way, and I would not gamble on it. The fix is sitting right there for the applicant: serve the documents again properly, or go to court and ask for the original service to be approved under Rule 6(18). Given that the documents are plainly in your hands, there is a real chance a court approves it. The steady approach is the one I keep coming back to: let your lawyer argue about the service, and on your side, count 30 days from the day you received the documents, prepare your Answer, and file it. Do not just wait.
