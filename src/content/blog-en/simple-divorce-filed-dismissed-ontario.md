Yes. Filing the application is only the beginning, not the end. If your case still isn't finished 365 days after it was started, the court sends a Notice of Approaching Dismissal. If you still do nothing 60 days after that notice, the clerk dismisses your entire case — with no further warning. No divorce certificate, and you're still married.

---

## 1. Filing Is Only the First Step

A [simple divorce](/en/blog/uncontested-divorce-ontario/) is a chain of steps: file the application, serve it on your spouse, wait out their 30 days to respond, file your affidavit for divorce and a draft divorce order, a judge reviews and signs, the divorce takes effect 31 days later, and then you can get your certificate.

Wherever the chain breaks, the case stops there.

The most common break is [service](/en/blog/uncontested-divorce-ontario/). The application has to reach your spouse first — either they sign for it, or a process server delivers it. If they won't cooperate or can't be found, that step never closes, and everything behind it stalls.

---

## 2. The Court Runs on a Clock: 365 Days, Then 60

The Family Law Rules are explicit. If a case has not been settled, withdrawn, or scheduled for trial within 365 days after it was started, the clerk must serve a Notice of Approaching Dismissal (Form 39), sent to the address or email you gave the court when you filed.

After that notice is served, you have 60 days. Within those 60 days you must actually accomplish one of these: obtain a court order lengthening the time, file a signed [agreement](/en/blog/separation-agreement-vs-divorce-ontario/) settling everything, withdraw the case, schedule the case for trial, or arrange a case conference.

Do none of them, and on day 60 the clerk signs a dismissal order and the whole case is gone — with no further notice to you.

One more point: time can only be lengthened by a court order. You and your spouse agreeing between yourselves to "take it slow" doesn't count.

---

## 3. What Happens If Your Case Is Dismissed

A dismissed case is as if you never applied. No [divorce certificate](/en/blog/uncontested-divorce-ontario/), and legally you are still married.

To get divorced, you start over: new application, new court fees, the whole process again.

In theory, you can bring a motion asking a judge to set aside the clerk's dismissal order. But that's an extra proceeding and extra legal fees, with no guarantee the judge agrees.

The worst version is this: the notice goes to the address and email on file from when you started the case. If you've moved, or the email lands in a folder you never check, your case can be dismissed without you ever knowing. The address in the court record is the one that counts — keep the contact details you filed with up to date and watch them.

---

## What to Do

If a Form 39 arrives, don't put it in a drawer: the 60 days run from the day it's served.
Find out which step you're stuck on. Most cases stall at service. If your spouse won't sign for the documents, hire a process server — it typically costs a few hundred dollars.
If your spouse can't be found, don't just wait: you can ask the court for an order for [substituted service](/en/blog/default-judgment-divorce-ontario/).
Keep the email you filed with the court active and check it: it's the only way the court reaches you.

## FAQs

### 1. Mine is only a simple divorce. Does this deadline still apply?

Yes. A divorce application is a case under the Family Law Rules. If it isn't completed within 365 days, you get the same Form 39. "Simple" only means nobody is contesting it — it doesn't mean there's no clock.

### 2. If I file the missing materials within the 60 days, am I fine?

Yes — as long as you genuinely complete the missing step: finish service, file the proof of service and the affidavit for divorce. Phoning to say "I'm working on it" doesn't count — you must file proper, compliant legal documents.

### 3. My case was already dismissed. Can it be saved?

You can bring a motion asking a judge to set aside the dismissal order, but it costs money, you have to persuade the judge, and success isn't guaranteed. If that fails, the only route is a fresh application — court fees paid again, process started from the beginning.
