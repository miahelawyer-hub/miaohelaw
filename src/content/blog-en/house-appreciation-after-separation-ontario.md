The house is in one name. The other party is not coming back for now. One parent stays with the children and keeps paying the mortgage. The market is soft. Must we start a divorce and equalization now? If we wait and the house recovers, is that gain still shared?

Two ledgers. One is [equalization of net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/) — locked to the [separation date](/en/blog/separation-date-vs-divorce-date-ontario/). The other is title. A name on the register is not the same entry as a line on the NFP statement. How title is held was never the same as how the house is equalized. See [title versus property division](/en/blog/matrimonial-home-title-vs-property-division-ontario/).

---

## After separation, if the house goes up, do you still split the gain?

Ask whose name is on title.

The [Family Law Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/laws/stat/rso-1990-c-f3/latest/rso-1990-c-f3.html) values net family property as of the valuation date — usually the day of separation. Each spouse lists assets minus debts that day, then half the difference is paid. Gains, losses, and wages that arise **after** that day generally stay off the statement. So “do we still split the increase” is not a fairness slogan. It is whether the house, after separation, is still an asset the two of you rise with together.

On **sole title**, equalization usually **does not recut the later gain**. Market value minus the mortgage as of the separation date sits on the titled spouse’s statement. If that spouse then carries the payments and the market recovers, the extra equity generally stays with them. What the other spouse can claim is equalization of the valuation-date table, not half of tomorrow’s list price.

**Joint title** does not freeze the house price. Both names remain on title, so the house is still both of yours after separation. If it goes up, you share the gain. If it goes down, you share the loss. Net proceeds on a sale follow title. The adjustment that often appears when **only one of you lives there** is occupation rent — not a locked valuation-date list price.

## Sole title and joint title are not the same ledger

| | Sole title | Joint title |
|---|---|---|
| House price after separation | Usually locked to the separation-date equity; later movement generally stays off NFP | **Not frozen at the separation date**; later gains and losses are borne together until a sale |
| Only one spouse lives there | Occupation rent is less common and needs its own evidence | The excluded co-owner may have an occupation-rent claim on the facts |
| Who pays the mortgage after | Often the occupant; a large principal paydown may be a separate accounting | Payments and taxes set off occupation rent and affect how net proceeds are divided |
| Can the off-title spouse block a sale | While you are still spouses, s. 21 may still block it | A co-owner can move for partition and sale |

Three situations:

**Sole title, you live there, you pay.** Equalization looks at the separation date. Later appreciation is usually not shared. A trust claim — a down payment from the other spouse’s parents, or years of payments from their account — is a different cause of action. It needs contribution and intention. See [parental gifts and trusts](/en/blog/resulting-trust-parental-gifts-ontario/) and [Pecore v. Pecore, 2007 SCC 17](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2007/2007scc17/2007scc17.html).

**Joint title, one spouse moves out, the other stays with the children.** Moving out does not surrender title. A later recovery still has their share in it; a later drop is shared as well. Paying the mortgage for three years does not buy the other half. Whether the occupant owes rent is the next section.

**A lump-sum principal paydown or a major renovation after separation.** Part of the later value is money put in afterwards. Courts sometimes deal with that through occupation rent, unjust enrichment, or equity. It is not automatically kept, and it is not automatically recut.

## Joint title, only one of you lives there: occupation rent?

It may arise. It is not automatic rent.

A jointly owned house is still both of yours. If one spouse occupies to the exclusion of the other, the excluded co-owner can claim occupation rent. It is a discretionary remedy, not nearby list rent multiplied by months. The Court of Appeal in [Griffiths v. Zambosco, 2001 CanLII 24097](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2001/2001canlii24097/2001canlii24097.html) treated amount and entitlement as a weighing exercise. The factor list in [Higgins v. Higgins, 2001 CanLII 28223](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onsc/doc/2001/2001canlii28223/2001canlii28223.html) is the one Ontario courts still walk through. The court usually looks at:

- who paid the mortgage, property tax, insurance and maintenance
- whether a minor child lived there
- how long the claim sat
- whether the excluded party ever moved for a sale, or simply stayed away without pushing a listing

An occupant who carried all the carrying costs and the children often sees the amount cut, sometimes to nothing. The other way around — occupying, keeping the other party out, refusing to sell, and still having them pay part of the mortgage — is a stronger occupation-rent case. Do not expect “you lived there rent-free” to win the entire house, and do not assume that paying the loan on a joint home means there is no rent claim.

Sale and occupation procedure is in [when a spouse will not sell, pay, or leave](/en/blog/force-sale-matrimonial-home-ontario/).

## If I start a claim now, will the court order a sale?

It may. It is not required.

When the parties cannot agree on value — one says one number, the other says another — a judge often cannot price the house on paper. A sale is then a common way to get a real price, with net proceeds held in trust for equalization. On joint title, a co-owner can already move under the [Partition Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/laws/stat/rso-1990-c-p4/latest/rso-1990-c-p4.html).

That is not “file, then list.” Minor children, housing stability, exclusive possession, and files with family violence or a restraining order often put who lives there ahead of an immediate sale. A sale order can set a move-out date, require cooperation with showings, give one party sole carriage of the listing, and freeze net proceeds without putting anyone on the street the same week.

While you are still spouses, a matrimonial home usually cannot be listed or remortgaged without the other spouse’s consent or a court order, **even if they are not on title** ([Family Law Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/laws/stat/rso-1990-c-f3/latest/rso-1990-c-f3.html) s. 21). They can still say it is the matrimonial home and block a unilateral sale. On sole title, what is locked is the separation-date equity, not the power to sell. On joint title, the house price was never frozen at the valuation date. After a recognized divorce, when someone is still living in a house in your name, that is a different article: [After the divorce, the ex is still in my house](/en/blog/after-divorce-ex-still-in-my-house-ontario/).

## Can I wait for the market to recover?

On sole title, where you occupy and pay, waiting can favour the titled owner. A low valuation date, then a recovery that stays off NFP, is the arithmetic. Joint title is the opposite: you wait on the same house together, so later gains and losses are not yours alone.

Do not treat “wait for the market” as a plan. Equalization is the earliest of three clocks under [Family Law Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/laws/stat/rso-1990-c-f3/latest/rso-1990-c-f3.html) s. 7(3): **two years** after divorce or nullity; **six years** after separation with no reasonable prospect of resuming cohabitation; **six months** after a spouse’s death. A divorce certificate first, property later, can start the two-year clock. Six years from separation can expire even without a divorce. Section 2(8) can extend time, but only with a prima facie case, delay in good faith, and no substantial prejudice — an exception, not a market-timing tool.

The separation date itself has to be proved. Living under one roof, or one spouse leaving because of a restraining order, can be a separation, if the intention is not to resume cohabitation. Affectionate messages and a return to the house push the date forward and recast the entire appreciation ledger.

Not starting a property case yet does not mean keeping no records. Bank statements, mortgage history, and assessments or tax bills around the separation date are the base of the later table. Child support is a different claim, available from separation. See [We separated but never sued. Can I still claim past child support?](/en/blog/retroactive-child-support-ontario/).

## Common questions

### After separation, if the house goes up, do you still split the gain?

It depends on title. Sole title: equalization usually locks the separation-date equity. Joint title: the house price is not frozen; later gains and losses are borne together.

### It is only my name. I paid. The market recovered. Can they still claim the gain?

Usually not on equalization. Trust, occupation rent, and a large post-separation paydown need their own evidence.

### What if we are joint?

Both names stay on title. The house price is not locked at the separation date. Gains and losses are shared. If only one of you lives there, occupation rent may arise.

### We are joint and only I live there. Do I owe rent?

Possibly, but it is not automatic rent. [Higgins](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onsc/doc/2001/2001canlii28223/2001canlii28223.html) looks at who paid the carrying costs, whether a child lived there, and how long the claim sat. The figure is often cut.

### If I sue now, will the house be sold?

It may. Not always. Children, housing, and safety often come before an immediate listing.

### Wait for the market?

On sole title it may help. On joint title you wait together. Limitation still runs. Do not bet s. 7(3) on a recovery.

### They are not on title. Can they block a sale?

While you are spouses, the matrimonial-home rules may still block a unilateral sale. The locked number and the power to sell are two questions.
