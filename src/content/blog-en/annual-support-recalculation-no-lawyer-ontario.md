No. The annual recalculation your agreement calls for is simply this: once a year, the new income goes into the child [support](/en/family-law/support/) guidelines and produces a new figure. It is an arithmetic exercise, not a new negotiation. There are only three things to do each year: exchange income information, work out the new figure, and confirm it in writing — and written confirmation does not have to be formal; an email counts. The child support figure itself comes out the same whether you use a free online calculator or a lawyer's paid software.

A client came to consult me. Her [separation agreement](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/) said child support would be recalculated every year based on income. As the first recalculation came due, she asked: do we have to hire a lawyer to do this every year? The way the clause is written, is this lawyer someone we keep forever?

The short answer: no. The recalculation is something you can handle yourselves.

---

## 1. A Recalculation Is Not a Renegotiation. The Rules Were Settled the Day the Agreement Was Signed

A lawyer's value was in that one signing: getting the rules right — in which month each year income is exchanged, on what basis the figure is calculated, and from which month the new figure takes effect. With those rules written into the agreement, each year's recalculation merely carries them out: income changed, so the figure changes. The rules haven't changed, so there is no need to pay someone every year to explain them again. Running a formula that was settled long ago, and negotiating an agreement, are two different things.

---

## 2. There Are Only Three Steps Each Year, and None of Them Needs a Lawyer in the Room

### Step One: Exchange Income

At the time the agreement sets, the paying spouse gives the other the income documents — the notice of assessment, the tax return, pay stubs; whatever the agreement specifies.

### Step Two: Calculate

There is a website built for this, called [My Support Calculator](https://www.mysupportcalculator.ca/), and the free version is quite enough: enter both incomes and the children's information, and it calculates the amount under the guidelines automatically — no need to work through the tables yourself.

### Step Three: Confirm in Writing

The two of you confirm the new figure: from which month, and how much per month. An email is enough for this — it is in writing, it is dated, and both of you keep it. That completes the step.

---

## 3. The Child Support Figure Is No More Accurate for Having Been Calculated by a Lawyer

Child support comes from the guidelines table fixed by law: this income, this many children, this province — the figure is what it is. Enter the same income into a free online calculator and into the paid software in a law office, and you get the same number, because both are reading the same table. The software's price is not buying a different answer. This calculation comes out the same no matter who runs it.

---

## 4. A Lawyer Is Needed Only When the Income Figure Itself Is in Dispute

Draw the line clearly: whether the annual recalculation can be handled without a lawyer turns not on the calculation, but on whether the income figure is disputed. If neither side disputes the other's income — the pay stubs and tax returns are on the table and the numbers are plain to see — the yearly adjustment can be finished entirely on your own; there is nothing for a lawyer to do. A lawyer becomes necessary in a different situation: one side believes the income the other reports is not real. That is no longer an arithmetic problem; it is a factual dispute — how much does he actually earn, does the money he takes from his corporation count as his income, can the figures reported for a self-employment business be believed. Disputes of that kind cannot be talked out between the two of you. They need a lawyer: to test the numbers, to require complete income disclosure from the other side, and, if agreement still fails, to use the court process to have the true income determined. So the test is simple: if the dispute is about how to calculate, settle it yourselves; if the dispute is about whether the income is real, [that is when you call a lawyer](/en/blog/imputed-income-child-support-ontario/). One further situation has nothing to do with recalculation: where what needs changing is not the figure but a term of the agreement itself — that is [renegotiating the agreement](/en/blog/separation-agreement-validity-ontario/), and it also calls for a lawyer.

## FAQs

### 1. When the recalculation comes due, he won't give me his income documents. What do I do?

The agreement says income is exchanged every year, so he is obliged to provide them. First, ask in writing — by email — so the request is on record. If he still won't, the matter is beyond self-calculation: a lawyer's letter, or a court process to compel disclosure of his income. Hiring a lawyer then is for that one matter.

### 2. We confirmed the new amount by email, and now he won't pay the new figure. What then?

The email confirmation is your written proof — keep it. If your support is enforced through the [FRO](/en/blog/separation-agreement-not-paying-child-support-fro-ontario/), report the change the two of you confirmed and update the registration through the proper process, so the FRO enforces the new figure. If it isn't with the FRO, that email is the evidence you rely on to claim the new amount.

### 3. Spousal support is recalculated annually under our agreement too. Can we handle that ourselves as well?

It depends on how the agreement wrote the rule. If the agreement sets out the method of recalculation clearly, you can calculate it yourselves and confirm it in writing, the same way. Child support is a table — the most mechanical of the calculations. For spousal support, if the agreement only says "to be adjusted by agreement each year" with no method stated, a disagreement will need a lawyer or a court. That is why writing the recalculation rule clearly, at the signing, saves trouble every year after.
