Cash taken on separation day, with no photos and no paper trail, can't be recovered. A court can't decide cash existed based on words alone — without proof, it has no basis to rule. Transfers from the [company account](/en/blog/company-in-my-name-never-looked-books-divorce-ontario/) that left records are a different story; those can be traced.

A client separated a few years ago. On the day of separation, her spouse emptied the cash from the home safe and left. She asked me: can I get that money back?

The short answer: no.

---

## 1. Making Sense Isn't Enough for a Court to Rule

Her story made complete sense — the family had run a small business for years, and the cash takings had been kept in the safe at home. On separation day, he took it all. It sounds entirely plausible. But a court doesn't decide on plausibility; it decides on [evidence](/en/blog/electronic-evidence-ontario-family-court/). How do you prove the cash was there? Photos? None. [Bank statements](/en/blog/spouse-kept-company-books-use-in-divorce-ontario/)? Cash never entered a bank account, so there are none. Video? None. If you can't even prove the money existed, how can a judge order it returned? A judge can't.

---

## 2. Cash Leaves No Trail

Bank transfers, cheques, and statements all leave records a judge can see. Cash doesn't. Worse, this cash was never reported for tax — so it doesn't appear in tax records either. What basis would a judge have to find that the money existed? Your word alone? Your word alone is not evidence.

---

## 3. Most People Lose on This Point Because of Everyday Habits

Cash gets spent as it comes in — no ledger, no photos, no video. Then separation comes, and when it's time to prove something in court, there's nothing to show. A court only recognizes what can be proven; a story, however reasonable, counts for nothing without evidence. That's not a judge being unreasonable — it's the rule of litigation: whoever makes the claim bears the burden of proof.

One practical step you can take right now: put a security camera where you keep valuables — the storage room, the room with the safe. This isn't only about separation. Friends visit, their children visit; an accident happens, or worse, a break-in — the footage is your evidence either way. And if you do separate one day, those recordings can show what valuables and cash were actually in the house, instead of leaving you with nothing but your word.

For money already gone with no evidence behind it, litigating is throwing good money after bad. Put your energy into what can be recovered — recorded transfers, [jointly owned property](/en/blog/matrimonial-home-title-vs-property-division-ontario/), the company — and claim all of that. And from today, keep records: receipts, payment records, photos for every significant amount. The evidence you preserve today is what you'll stand on in court tomorrow.

## FAQs

### 1. I can prove we owned a safe. Does that prove there was money in it?

No. Proving the safe existed doesn't prove money was in it, or how much. The judge needs evidence the money existed, not that the box existed.

### 2. The cash was never reported for tax. Does that make it harder?

Yes. Unreported income doesn't appear in tax records, so there's one less place for the court to find it.

### 3. Why can company account transfers be recovered?

Because they left records. The company was jointly owned, and the books clearly show who took what. One person can't take all of a jointly owned company's money.
