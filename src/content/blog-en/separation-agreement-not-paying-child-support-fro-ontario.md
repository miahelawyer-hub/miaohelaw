You can file the [separation agreement](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/) with the FRO for enforcement — you don't have to sue him in court first. As long as the agreement states a monthly child [support](/en/family-law/support/) amount, once the agreement is filed with the court and registered with the FRO, the FRO will enforce it exactly as it enforces a court order: deductions, payments, and enforcement against arrears, all of it.

A client came to consult me. She and her former spouse had signed a separation agreement that included child support. For a while after signing, he paid every month. Then he stopped, and several months went by. Her question: this is just an agreement the two of us signed — it isn't a court order. If he won't pay, is my only option to take him to court?

The short answer: no. File the agreement with the FRO, and it can be enforced.

---

## 1. At the FRO, an Agreement Is Enforced the Same Way as an Order

Many people assume the FRO only enforces court orders. It doesn't. A separation agreement is a domestic contract, and once it has been filed with the court and registered with the FRO as the law requires, its child support and spousal support terms carry the same enforcement force as a court order. [Wages are garnished](/en/blog/spousal-child-support-ontario/) the same way, arrears are pursued the same way. The FRO does not do less because what you hold is an agreement.

---

## 2. What Matters Is That the Amount Is Written Clearly in the Agreement

The FRO enforces the number on the paper, so whether it can take your case comes down to what the agreement says: how much is payable each month, and when. If that is written clearly, the FRO can enforce it. If the agreement only says "support in accordance with the Child Support Guidelines" with no actual figure, or says nothing about support at all, there is no number for the FRO to enforce. This is why, when an agreement is being signed, the support term must be a definite amount — not a principle.

A warning: the agreement must be a proper agreement, drafted by a lawyer and formally signed. The child support amount has to be calculated from the payor's income under the Child Support Guidelines and set out clearly — the amount itself, and how often it is paid. Some agreements are written and signed by the parties themselves, stating only that one side "shall pay child support" — no monthly amount, and no payment frequency: monthly? weekly? Nothing at all. A term written that way cannot be enforced by the FRO; it is as though it was never written. And there is a more basic problem underneath: to be [enforceable](/en/blog/separation-agreement-validity-ontario/) at all, a domestic contract must be in writing, signed by the parties, and witnessed. Something written out on a sheet of paper, signed by the two of you, with no witness, does not stand as an agreement in the first place — let alone one the FRO can enforce.

---

## 3. Arrears That Have Already Built Up Go In at the Same Time and Are Pursued Together

When you register, file a [Statement of Arrears](/en/blog/fro-monthly-paying-arrears-still-collect-ontario/) for what he already owes — a sworn statement listing, item by item, how much is owed and for which months. Once the FRO opens the account, the old arrears and the ongoing monthly amount are enforced together. There is no reason to wait until he owes more before you act.

---

## 4. If He Has No Wages to Garnish, or He Has Left Ontario, the FRO Is Not Enough — Sue Promptly

The FRO's strongest tools are wage deductions and interception of federal payments, and both depend on his having a source of income in Ontario. If he has no wages and no steady income, or he has left Ontario altogether, FRO enforcement slows down — and may not reach him at all. But as long as he still has assets or real estate in Ontario, there is another route: see a lawyer promptly and sue in court. First, ask the court to freeze his Ontario property, so he cannot transfer or sell it. Then ask the court to order him to pay all of the arrears in one lump sum, and to have the support payable in the future dealt with as a lump sum as well, payable out of that property. The one thing not to do in this situation is wait: if you sue only after he has disposed of everything he had in Ontario, you can win the case and still collect nothing.

## The Filing Process

### Step One: File the Agreement with the Court

Don't let this step scare you. It is simple. Filing is not suing. There is no hearing, no judge reviewing the content of your agreement, and your former spouse does not have to attend or consent. There are just three small things to do. First, complete Form 26B — the affidavit for filing a domestic contract. It is one page: it identifies the parties and the date of the agreement, and you swear that the agreement is in effect and has not been set aside or changed by a court or by a later agreement; the agreement is attached behind it. You don't need to find someone to swear it for you — family court offices have commissioners who will commission family forms for free. Second, take the form and the agreement to the court: you can file in person at the counter of an Ontario court — the Ontario Court of Justice or the Family Court branch of the Superior Court of Justice — or you can file online. It does not matter if you have never had a case and have no court file number: filing is simply putting the agreement on record with the court; you do not need a lawsuit under way first. Third, the court accepts the documents, stamps them, and keeps them on file — and that is the step complete. Once filed, the support terms of the agreement carry the same force as a court order. The whole thing is one form and one filing: the lightest step in the entire process.

### Step Two: Register with the FRO

Send the FRO a copy of the filed agreement along with the FRO's registration materials. The FRO opens a case and an account showing the amount payable each month and when it is due.

### Step Three: Give the FRO Complete Information About the Payor

Where he works, where his income comes from, how he can be reached — provide everything you know. Wage deductions and locating him depend on this information; the more complete it is, the faster enforcement moves.

### Step Four: File the Statement of Arrears

Arrears that accumulated before registration are listed item by item, with their months, sworn to, and filed with the FRO, to be enforced together with the ongoing monthly amount.

### Step Five: Watch the Statements

Once the case is open, the FRO issues statements of account. Check each one — what was due, what was paid, what remains owing — and if a figure is wrong, contact the FRO promptly to have it corrected.

## FAQs

### 1. Our agreement was just signed between us and never went near a court. Can it still be filed with the FRO?

Yes. Filing with the court is step one of the process: file the agreement with the Form 26B affidavit first, then register it with the FRO. The agreement itself must meet the basic formalities of a domestic contract — in writing, signed by both parties, and witnessed. If there is a defect in the formalities, have a lawyer look at it first to see whether it can be fixed.

### 2. He says a private agreement doesn't count and he doesn't have to pay. Is he right?

No. Once the agreement is filed and registered, its support terms are enforced like a court order, and whether he pays is not his choice. If he believes the amount is no longer reasonable, his only route is to apply to court to change it — and until a court does, the FRO enforces the figure written in the agreement.

### 3. Our agreement only says support is payable "in accordance with the Guidelines," with no specific amount. What now?

The FRO needs a definite number to enforce. First, see whether the amount can be calculated from his income and the Child Support Guidelines and written in by agreement between you. If you can't agree, the amount has to be set by a court — after which the FRO enforces it. And for anyone signing an agreement now: write this term as a specific monthly amount.
