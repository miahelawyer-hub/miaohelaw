The line that comes into a consultation is: we already divorced in China. The judgment ended the marriage and named a parent for the child. The house in Ontario was not mentioned. The other party has now issued in Ontario and wants a share. Is that still open?

Often it is. The path is not a second Canadian divorce. It is [equalization of net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/). Recognizing the foreign divorce, and still dividing property the foreign court never touched, are different questions. Recognition is in [China–Canada divorce judgments](/en/blog/china-canada-divorce-judgment-recognition/).

---

## Divorced in China — can Ontario still divide the property?

Often yes.

[Divorce Act s. 22](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/d-3.4/) says a foreign divorce that meets the statutory conditions is to be recognized — typically, that either former spouse was ordinarily resident in that country for at least one year immediately before the proceeding started. After recognition, Ontario will not grant another divorce, and it will not treat the foreign divorce as a Canadian one so that Divorce Act corollary relief can be added on.

Property is a different statute. [Cheng v. Liu](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2017/2017onca104/2017onca104.html) is clear: after a valid foreign divorce, Ontario can still determine **equalization** and **child support** under the Family Law Act. A Canadian house or account that the Chinese court did not divide is not, for that reason alone, legally finished.

If the other party still holds a home in China, Ontario values worldwide net family property and uses set-off. It does not send someone to Guangzhou to execute. How both houses enter the same statement is on the [cross-border assets](/en/family-law/cross-border-assets/) page.

Cheng is about **jurisdiction**. A valid foreign divorce does not close Ontario's mouth on property the foreign court never touched. Divorce Act corollary relief — adding property or support onto a Canadian divorce order — is closed. Part I equalization under the Family Law Act is not. A consultation note that says only "after a Chinese divorce the Divorce Act has no jurisdiction" is at most half right. That sentence is about the divorce order and the support path, not the house.

## Does a civil-affairs divorce count the same as a court judgment?

Ask first whether it is a divorce granted by a competent foreign authority.

In China some people obtain a court judgment; others register a divorce at civil affairs. The paper in hand may be a divorce certificate, a mediation statement, or a judgment. Ontario still asks: has a foreign authority with jurisdiction ended the marriage, and did that instrument deal with Canadian property. If s. 22 is met, you usually do not divorce twice in Ontario. Property left off the page can still be equalized on its own.

A civil-affairs divorce is where "end the marriage first, property later" shows up most often. Later, in Ontario, is limitation running. Notarizing and translating the certificate proves status. It does not prove the Canadian house was divided.

## If the judgment never mentioned the Canadian house, did we give it up?

No. Silence usually means no claim was made, or the Chinese court would not deal with foreign immovable property. Back in Ontario, the asset can still enter net family property.

What actually kills the claim is [limitation](/en/blog/equalization-limitation-chinese-lawyer-ontario/), not a blank in the judgment. Section 7(3) runs to the earliest of: **two years** after divorce or nullity; **six years** after separation with no reasonable prospect of resuming cohabitation; **six months** after a spouse's death. A recognized foreign divorce can start the two-year clock from the date on that certificate.

Waiting because “we divorced in China and never touched the Canadian house, so we can divide it whenever” is how both the two-year and the six-year lines are missed.

Expiry is not automatic death. The court may extend under s. 2(8) if there is a prima facie case, the delay was in good faith, and no one would suffer substantial prejudice. That is exceptional relief. It is not a planning tool.

## The Chinese judgment has no separation date. How is valuation set?

Ontario looks at the [date of separation](/en/blog/separation-date-vs-divorce-date-ontario/), not the date on the Chinese certificate. Net family property is locked to separation. The limitation clocks may run from separation, divorce, or death. Those are not the same date.

A recital that the parties were “separated under one roof” without a day still leaves Ontario to find a day. The foreign narrative is evidence. It is not the finding. Years later, when banks no longer keep old statements, that day is often unprovable. Fail to prove an early separation, and the six-year line may not help. The two-year line from the divorce date may already have expired.

Ontario does recognize separation under one roof. It requires evidence. The Chinese habit of “divorce first, property later” does not fill in Ontario’s valuation date.

If the Chinese court already divided some assets — domestic deposits, say, but not the Toronto house — Ontario will not usually recompute the portions that were settled and that it recognizes. The item that never went into the judgment can still enter NFP. Whether the "whole case is overturned" depends on the scope of recognition and on whether disclosure was complete. It is not "China divorced, so Canada starts over," and it is not "silence forever bars a claim."

## What should you gather if you have already been served?

A Form 8 does not stop because "we already divorced in China." The Answer still has a deadline. The documents that usually matter are:

- the Chinese judgment, mediation statement or divorce certificate, with translation and notarization
- the application and financial statement (Form 13.1), to see whether the claim is equalization, support, housing or a restraining order
- title, the purchase agreement, mortgage and property-tax records for the Canadian house
- evidence of the separation date: a move, split accounts, a second lease, travel records, contemporaneous messages — bank statements from thirteen years ago are often gone
- whatever can be disclosed about a house or account in China, so set-off can be done

Until recognition, valuation and limitation are mapped, the later numbers are empty. If someone is still living in the house, see [After the divorce, the ex is still in my house](/en/blog/after-divorce-ex-still-in-my-house-ontario/). Spousal support is a different path: [Can you avoid Ontario spousal support by divorcing abroad?](/en/blog/foreign-divorce-avoid-spousal-support-ontario/).

## Common questions

### Divorced in China — can Ontario still divide the property?

Often yes. After recognition you do not divorce twice. Untouched Canadian property can still be equalized. Cheng v. Liu is the authority.

### If the judgment was silent on the house, did we give it up?

No. Silence is not a release. The risk is s. 7(3).

### Does Ontario treat the Chinese certificate as the divorce date automatically?

A s. 22 divorce is generally recognized. A foreign divorce obtained to evade Ontario obligations can be refused. Property and support are not the same analysis.

### We separated years ago. Are we out of time?

Not always, but the risk is high. Earliest of the three clocks. Section 2(8) is an exception.

### The judgment recites a separation but no date. What then?

Ontario still needs a date. Without one, both the NFP statement and the limitation analysis are unstable.

### Does a civil-affairs divorce still leave property open in Ontario?

Recognition and whether Canadian property was dealt with still control. A house or account left out of the agreement can still be equalized, and the two-year clock may already be running.

### Will Ontario execute against a house in China?

Usually it will not send anyone to transfer title there. Worldwide NFP uses set-off. Execution falls on assets in Canada.

### If China already divided some property, will Ontario recompute everything?

It depends on what the judgment did. Settled and recognized portions are not usually reopened. Canadian property that never went in can still be equalized on its own.
