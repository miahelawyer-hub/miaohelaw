The child says they do not want to be alone with the other parent. They want you there, or another child in the house. The other parent refuses. A case is already before the court. Can you ask for supervised parenting time?

Two ledgers. One is [decision-making and parenting time](/en/blog/child-custody-ontario-chinese/) — best interests only. The other is a restriction: whether a third person must be in the room. The child’s reluctance is a factor. It is not a switch. Where a restraining order is already on the file, the temporary starting point is usually supervision, not unsupervised time.

---

## If the child does not want to see the other parent alone, can you require supervised parenting time?

You can ask. It is not automatic.

Section 16 of the [Divorce Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/laws/stat/rsc-1985-c-3-2nd-supp/latest/rsc-1985-c-3-2nd-supp.html) and s. 24 of the [Children’s Law Reform Act](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/laws/stat/rso-1990-c-c12/latest/rso-1990-c-c12.html) both require the court to consider the child’s views and preferences, giving them weight in line with age and maturity. Neither section says that a child’s refusal of unsupervised time means every visit must be watched.

Two tracks:

**No restraining order, no proven risk to the child, only a stated reluctance.** Supervised parenting time remains the exception. The usual justifications are harm or a risk of harm, abduction, substance abuse, serious clinical issues, or reintroduction after a long absence. “I do not want to go alone” does not, by itself, usually reach that threshold.

**A restraining order is already in place.** The court will not grant unsupervised parenting time on day one. The temporary order is usually supervised time: the child still sees the parent; the adults do not meet. That is not a finding that parental rights are gone. It is a frame that can be carried out. The next section.

Supervision cuts ordinary parent–child time: short visits, a confined setting, a third person present. The Court of Appeal in [M. (B.P.) v. M. (B.L.D.E.), 1992 CanLII 8642](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/1992/1992canlii8642/1992canlii8642.html) described supervised contact as temporary and time-limited — a way through an impasse, **not a long-term plan**. [Izyuk v. Bilousov, 2015 ONSC 3684](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onsc/doc/2015/2015onsc3684/2015onsc3684.html) put the same point on parenting time: the hope is that the problem that justified supervision can be addressed, and a more natural relationship can resume. Do not read supervision as “watched visits until the child is an adult.”

## The court will not grant ordinary parenting time on day one; with a restraining order, supervision is the usual first step

On a temporary motion or a first appearance, the court wants an order that can be carried out and that keeps the child safe. Facts are still in dispute. Disclosure is incomplete. **It will not grant equal, unsupervised parenting time at the outset, and it will not make a final custody order that day.**

A [restraining order](/en/blog/ontario-restraining-order-chinese-family/) makes that caution sharper. The order governs the adults: who may not approach whom, whether they may call, whether they may meet at an exchange. It does **not** mean the other parent has no rights in the child. In practice, a file with a restraining order usually starts with **supervised parenting time** (sometimes a supervised exchange): a third person present, or a designated centre, so the adults do not meet. Not zero contact. Not leaving the child alone with the restrained parent.

The restraining order protects the applicant. Whether the child is also at risk is a separate evidentiary question. Violence or threats directed at the child can tighten the order further; a complete pause on contact is the extreme. Do not read “there is a restraining order” as “the other parent never sees the child,” and do not read it as “overnight weekends at the first hearing.”

## How the court hears the child: start with age

The statutes do not freeze a birthday. In practice, age is the first screen.

**Too young.** The court will usually not put the child in the witness box, and will not treat “they told me they do not want to go” in one parent’s affidavit as a deciding factor. A young child often cannot separate discomfort from loyalty to the parent who brought them. A parent’s retelling is the parent’s evidence, not the child’s.

**An adolescent.** The court will listen. The Office of the Children’s Lawyer, a Voice of the Child report, sometimes a judicial interview, are the usual channels. Hearing is not obeying. The wish has to be clear, stable, and informed, and the court still asks whether a parent shaped it. A teenager who says “I will not go alone” is still placed in the whole file: safety, day-to-day care, each parent’s capacity, how the relationship actually ran. The child does not write the order.

The Court of Appeal in [Decaen v. Decaen, 2013 ONCA 218](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2013/2013onca218/2013onca218.html) listed what to weigh when assessing a child’s wishes: whether both parents can provide adequate care; how clear the wish is; how informed it is; age; maturity; strength of the wish; how long it has been expressed; practicalities; parental influence; overall context. Age is one item on that list. [Stefureak v. Chambers, 2004 CanLII 34521](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onsc/doc/2004/2004canlii34521/2004canlii34521.html) said the same in different words: weight depends on age, intelligence, apparent maturity, and the child’s ability to articulate a view.

Do not plan around “twelve years old, the child decides.” There is also no hard bar that “under ten, the court never hears them.” Too young, not decisive. Older, heard — still the whole file.

## Third-party evidence is more reliable than your retelling

On whether the child wants contact, the court wants an **independent source**, not one party’s paraphrase.

You and the other parent are litigants. When the stories conflict, a judge cannot infer the child’s mind from competing WeChat threads and affidavits. Chat logs may show a fight, and that the child was present. They do not prove that professional supervision is required. How electronic records get into court is in [WeChat, screenshots, and recordings](/en/blog/electronic-evidence-ontario-family-court/).

What carries more weight:

- a psychologist, or another qualified assessment
- a social worker
- the Office of the Children’s Lawyer, counsel or a clinical investigator
- a Voice of the Child report
- existing school or family-doctor records that already speak to risk around contact — not a last-minute letter you asked a teacher to write

Those professionals are not stenographers for the child’s last sentence. They look at consistency, rehearsal, and whether the child’s age can support the judgment. A counsellor who only heard your side, hired for the motion, is a weak exhibit. An ongoing treating relationship, or someone the OCL assigned, looks like evidence.

Where safety is the issue — violence, stalking, loss of control in front of the child — start with a [restraining order](/en/blog/ontario-restraining-order-chinese-family/). Once that order is in place, parenting time usually lands on supervision first. Do not expect one sentence from the child to do that work. A restraining order and parenting time can run together: contact between the adults is blocked; the child’s time with the other parent moves onto a supervised track. It does not automatically go to zero.

## A relative in the room is not court-ordered supervision

Three arrangements:

**Professional supervision.** A designated centre or a neutral supervisor. Short visits. A record. That is usually what “supervised parenting time” means on an application.

**You, or another child in the household, sitting in.** That is your condition, not the court’s supervisor. The other parent will say your presence is control. Courts rarely make “the primary parent must sit beside them” a long-term term.

**Non-disparagement.** If the other parent said things they should not have said in front of the child, the usual order binds both of you: do not denigrate, do not brief the child on the case, sometimes a parenting program. That does not automatically upgrade every visit into a watched hour.

Where a parent has been absent and the child needs to be reintroduced, supervision is sometimes a **step out**, not a wall. Where contact is already happening and the only new fact is “the child says they are uncomfortable,” cutting time to two supervised hours a week invites the question: where is the risk, and who wrote it down besides you.

## Common questions

### If the child does not want to go alone, can you require supervised parenting time?

You can ask. It is not automatic. Age, the whole file, and independent evidence are read together.

### At what age will the court listen?

No frozen number. Too young, usually not decisive. A teenager will be heard, and still does not write the order. See [Decaen](https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2013/2013onca218/2013onca218.html).

### The child told me they do not want to go. Is an affidavit enough?

Usually not. A psychologist, a social worker, or the OCL is a stronger source than a parent’s retelling.

### If a sibling sits in, is that supervision?

Generally no. The court wants a neutral third party, not a family member in the chair.

### No violence — only reluctance?

Rarely a basis for long-term supervision. Without a restraining order, supervision is the exception, not the default.

### There is a restraining order. Can the other parent still see the child?

Usually yes, on a supervised basis. A restraining order does not erase parental rights, and it is not unsupervised time on day one.

### Will the first hearing grant equal parenting time?

No. Temporary orders stay cautious. With a restraining order, supervision is the usual first step.

### They speak badly of me in front of the child. Is that enough?

Not automatically. Non-disparagement is the more common order. Without a restraining order, turning time into supervision still needs independent evidence of risk.
