No. The amount written in the agreement stays the amount until one of two things happens: the two of you reach a new agreement, or the court makes an order changing it. Being laid off changes your circumstances; it does not change the number on the page. Every dollar you pay short in the meantime is [arrears](/en/blog/fro-monthly-paying-arrears-still-collect-ontario/).

A client came to consult me. He and his former spouse had signed a [separation agreement](/en/family-law/domestic-contracts/), with [child support](/en/family-law/support/) and spousal support set out in it. Then he was laid off and his income stopped. His question: with no job, surely the amount in the agreement doesn't still apply in full? Can I pay a lower amount for now, and sort it out when I find work?

The short answer: no, it does not go down automatically. Pay less on your own, and the shortfall is a debt.

---

## 1. An Agreement Runs on What Is Written. Your Circumstances Changed; the Agreement Doesn't Change Itself

Unemployment is a change in your life. The figure in the agreement does not adjust itself to meet it. Until a new agreement is signed or a court order is made, the original amount remains in force: pay less, and the difference is arrears, which your former spouse can [file for enforcement](/en/blog/separation-agreement-not-paying-child-support-fro-ontario/) or collect later, all at once. Many people assume: "I genuinely have no income, so paying less must be acceptable." The law is not that the amount can never change — it is that there is a procedure for changing it: either the two of you negotiate and reach an agreement to lower the figure, or you bring a motion to change and let the court change it. Until one of those has happened, the figure in the agreement stands — and if you pay less on your own in the meantime, the shortfall is arrears.

---

## 2. If Your Former Spouse Agrees, the Two of You Can Adjust It Yourselves

Talk first. If she agrees to lower the amount, put the new arrangement in writing: from which month, how much, and what happens when you find work again — all of it written down. Her likely reason for refusing is the fear that once the amount goes down it never comes back; write in a mechanism — [recalculation](/en/blog/annual-support-recalculation-no-lawyer-ontario/) on your new income once you are working again — and agreement becomes much easier to reach. Where there is no dispute about the income figures and the two of you can settle it, this can be done entirely between you, with no court involved.

---

## 3. If She Doesn't Agree, You Apply to Court to Change the Amount — a Motion to Change

The court will not lower the figure simply because you lost your job. It looks mainly at three things.

First, whether the job loss is real and your income has actually fallen. The layoff letter, the termination documents, your employment insurance application — you must be able to produce them.

Second — and this is the point the court weighs most — whether you are actively looking for work. How many applications you have sent, which interviews you have attended, whether you have registered with employment agencies, whether you are retraining: the court looks at concrete action. If you are sitting at home, or turning down work that doesn't suit you, the court can treat you as earning what you were capable of earning — [income is imputed to you](/en/blog/imputed-income-child-support-ontario/) — and the amount stays where it was, as if you had never applied. So from the day you are laid off, keep records of the job search. They are the strongest evidence in your application.

Third, employment insurance benefits count as income. Receiving EI is not having no income: child support is recalculated on your EI amount under the guidelines — it goes down, it does not stop. Spousal support is likewise reassessed on the income you actually have.

And a point about timing: act as soon as the change happens. If you delay while paying less, the arrears pile up month by month, and by the time the court hears the matter, what to do with that pile is a second dispute of its own. Acting immediately does something more: the person who, the moment he loses his job, first goes to negotiate and, when negotiation fails, promptly brings the motion, is showing the court his attitude — that he is actively solving the problem and negotiating in good faith, not stalling on his payments. On a motion to change, the court looks not only at what your income is now, but at how you have handled the matter all along.

## FAQs

### 1. Can I pay the lower amount for now and make up the difference when I find work?

Without her written agreement and without a court order, no. Making it up later may be your intention, but on the books every short payment is arrears, claimable at any time, potentially with interest. The order of operations is the reverse: reach the agreement or bring the application first, then pay the new figure.

### 2. The court wants me "actively looking for work." How much is enough — is there a standard number of days?

There is no fixed number of days; the court looks at the conduct itself. Application records, interview invitations, registration with employment agencies, enrolment in training — the more concrete and continuous, the more they show a genuine search. Start keeping these records from the first day of unemployment. They cannot be reconstructed later when you suddenly need them.

### 3. She agreed to lower it. Is a verbal OK enough, or do we sign again?

Put it in writing. Write down the new amount, the month it starts, and how it will be recalculated or restored when you are working again, and both of you sign. A spoken "pay that for now" leaves you with nothing if she later denies agreeing. If the support runs through the [FRO](/en/blog/separation-agreement-not-paying-child-support-fro-ontario/), the new agreement also has to be updated with the FRO through the proper process before the FRO will act on the new figure.
