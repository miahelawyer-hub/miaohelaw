Yes. The payments arriving each month are the current [support](/en/family-law/support/). The stretch he missed before sits on the Family Responsibility Office's books as arrears, and the FRO will keep enforcing it — it is not wiped away just because he has started paying on time. The FRO stops enforcing arrears in only two situations: the court makes an order changing them, or both of you sign a written agreement consenting to the FRO not enforcing them.

A client came to consult me. Support between her and her former spouse runs through the FRO. For a period of time he hadn't paid, and arrears built up. Recently, he started paying again, month by month. Her worry: now that he is paying regularly, is the amount he owed from before simply gone?

The short answer: no. Arrears with the FRO will be enforced. You don't need to worry.

---

## 1. The FRO Keeps One Running Ledger, and Current Support and Arrears Are Recorded Separately

His payments arriving on time now means the current month's support is flowing and the arrears have stopped growing. It does not clear the old balance: the amount he owed from before stays on the FRO's books as a separate arrears figure, and it is not offset by his paying properly afterward. Current is current; the old debt is the old debt.

One order of things is worth knowing: payments the FRO receives are applied in a set order — current support first, then the older arrears, then interest. The money he pays now goes to the current month first, and only what is left over is applied to the old balance. That is exactly why the current support can be fully paid while the arrears shrink slowly: the arrears are not forgotten; they are being worked down, in line.

---

## 2. Enforcing Arrears Is the FRO's Job — But First You File a Statement of Arrears

The one step you must take is to give the FRO a Statement of Arrears. It is a sworn statement: how much is owed, for which months, and what you actually received during that period, set out item by item, sworn by you to be true, and filed with the FRO. Once the FRO has it, the arrears are recorded in that amount and enforced — you don't have to chase him yourself.

One point of scope: the FRO enforces more than court orders. The child support and spousal support terms of a [separation agreement](/en/blog/separation-agreement-not-paying-child-support-fro-ontario/), once the agreement has been filed with the court and registered with the FRO, are enforced in exactly the same way — and arrears under an agreement are claimed with the same Statement of Arrears.

Why does it have to be itemized in full? Because the FRO enforces the numbers on its own books, and some arrears it cannot know about on its own — amounts that fell owing before the order or agreement was registered with the FRO, for example. Those have to be claimed by you in the Statement of Arrears. If you don't report them, the FRO doesn't know they exist, and nobody chases them. On top of the principal, arrears can also carry interest, claimed in the same statement: the longer the arrears sit, the more, not less, he will ultimately have to pay.

Once the statement is filed, the FRO's enforcement tools escalate in stages: [deduction from wages](/en/blog/spousal-child-support-ontario/) first; then interception of federal money — tax refunds, employment insurance, pension benefits; then garnishment of bank accounts (including his half of a joint account) and reporting the arrears to credit bureaus; then suspension of his driver's licence (after 30 days' notice), his passport and other federal licences, and a lien registered against his property; and finally a default hearing in court, where a judge can impose fines — and, in serious cases, jail. The longer the arrears sit and the larger they grow, the heavier the tools become.

---

## 3. Arrears Don't Disappear on Their Own. Only Two Things Stop the Enforcement

Be clear about one thing first: the FRO has no power to reduce anything — not the current amount, and not the arrears. The most it can do is work out a payment plan with the payor. Actually reducing the amount, or wiping out arrears, can happen in only two ways. The first: he applies to court for an order changing the support, and the court actually changes the amount or the arrears. Applying is not the same as getting it — the court will look at why he didn't pay at the time and what his income and circumstances are now, and the decision is the court's. The second: the two of you sign a written agreement consenting to the FRO not enforcing that part of the arrears. Short of one of these two, his saying "I'm paying on time now — let the old amount go" has no effect, and neither does your agreeing to it verbally on the phone. The FRO acts on court orders and on written agreements between the parties, not on spoken words.

## FAQs

### 1. What do I have to do for the FRO to go after the arrears from before?

You file a Statement of Arrears with the FRO — a sworn statement listing, item by item, the amount owed and the months it covers, which you swear to be accurate. Once it is filed, the FRO enforces the arrears in that amount; the rest is its job. After that, check the FRO's statements regularly to make sure the figures are recorded correctly, and if something is off, contact the FRO promptly to have it corrected.

### 2. The arrears are from several years ago. Can they still be enforced?

Yes. Arrears don't clear themselves from the FRO's books with the passage of time — years old, they are still recorded and still enforced. The only things that stop the enforcement are the same two: a court order changing them, or both parties' written agreement.

### 3. He talked me into letting the old arrears go, and I said yes verbally. Can I change my mind?

A verbal yes doesn't stop the FRO. If you have not signed a written consent and no court has changed the order, the arrears are still being enforced. One caution: don't agree to this casually. The arrears are the child's support money. If you are ever going to [give them up](/en/blog/child-support-waiver-separation-agreement-ontario/), think it through first — and get legal advice before you sign anything.
