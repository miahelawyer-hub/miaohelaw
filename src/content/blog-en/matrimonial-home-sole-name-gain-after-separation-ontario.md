Yours. Ontario calculates [equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/) on the value as of the date of separation. Whatever the house does after that, up or down, has nothing to do with the other side. If it was high on separation day and sells low now, you bear the difference, and vice versa. Only a [jointly owned](/en/blog/house-appreciation-after-separation-ontario/) home shares the loss.

A client came in for advice. The matrimonial home is in her sole name. She and her husband have separated, and house prices have shifted since then. She wanted to know: if the house is worth more now than it was on the day they separated, does her husband share in the gain? And if it is worth less, does the loss fall on her alone?

The gains and losses after the separation date all belong to the titled owner.

---

## 1. What the law says

Under the [Family Law Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90f03), everything turns on one date: the valuation date. In a separation, section 4(1) sets that date as the day the spouses separate with no reasonable prospect of getting back together. Each spouse's [net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/) is measured on that day: everything they own, minus what they owe. Section 5(1) then gives the spouse with the smaller net family property half the difference. So the home counts at its separation-day value, in the titled spouse's column. Whatever happens after that day is not part of the calculation.

And this is not just about the matrimonial home. Changes to any other property after separation are left out too. The only exception is property the spouses own together. If both names are on it, both share what happens to it after separation.

---

## 2. The separation-day value

Think of the separation date as a photograph. The house is valued as of that day, and that value is locked in. What the house sells for later makes no difference. Say the market was high when the couple separated and low by the time the house finally sells. The equalization is still based on the high value, and the owner bears the loss. If the market moves the other way, the owner keeps the gain. The eventual sale price does not rewrite the equalization.

If the two sides disagree on that value, each side can hire their own appraiser. Both appraisers testify in court, and the judge accepts whichever appraisal is more reasonable. If both sides can agree on a value, the agreed value governs.

---

## 3. The gain and the loss, and the resulting trust claim

The rule cuts both ways. If the house goes up after separation, the titled owner keeps the whole gain. If it goes down, the titled owner bears the whole loss. The other spouse shares in neither, because they are not on title.

This is exactly why so many spouses claim a [resulting trust](/en/blog/resulting-trust-parental-gifts-ontario/) interest even when their name is not on the home. Equalization alone leaves them out of everything that happens after separation. But if they can prove they put money into the purchase or into the home, they can argue the titled spouse holds part of it in trust for them. A trust interest is a share of the property itself, so it shares in the appreciation too. Of course, if the home has lost value, the other side will not claim a resulting trust. They do not want to bear the loss. When the home is in one name only, this is the claim that follows. But a claim is not a win. It needs proof of contribution, and it stands or falls on the evidence.

---

## 4. A common misunderstanding: it makes no difference whose name is on the home

Many people assume the matrimonial home is divided the same way no matter whose name is on title. It is not. If both names are on title, the gain and the loss are shared. If only one name is on title, the other spouse's share is fixed on separation day, and everything after that belongs to the owner.

This happened in a real case. When the litigation started, the home's net value was only $200,000. The case went on for five years. By the time it ended, the home in the husband's sole name had gone up by $500,000. That $500,000 was not divided. It all went to the husband.

## Frequently asked questions

### 1. How is the separation-day value decided?

By a retrospective appraisal. An appraiser values the home as of the separation date, using the market as it was then.

### 2. What if the home is jointly owned?

Then the gain and the loss are shared. Both names are on title, so each spouse owns a share, and each share moves with the market. The appreciation or the drop after separation is borne by both, in proportion to their shares.

### 3. What if the other side claims a resulting trust?

A claim needs proof. The other side has to show they contributed money to the purchase or to the property, with records to back it up. A resulting trust turns on money put into the house itself. Without proof of contribution, the claim does not stand.
