Not enough. A [gift letter](/en/blog/chinese-divorce-property-gift-loan-trust-ontario/) is only one of three things you need. You also need the transfer records, and the money must have stayed in its own account the whole time. A letter alone, with the money mixed in, will not hold up the exclusion.

A client came in for advice. After the marriage, his parents send him money every year. He wants to keep that money separate from the family property, as his own. His parents are willing to sign a gift letter saying the money is for him alone. He asked: is the letter enough?

You need a gift letter, but it is not enough.

---

## What the Law Says

Section 4(2) of the Family Law Act says that property acquired by gift or inheritance from a third person after the date of the marriage does not form part of a spouse's [net family property](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/). Property into which the gift can be traced is excluded too. But there are two conditions: the property cannot be a [matrimonial home](/en/blog/prenup-excluded-house-became-matrimonial-home-ontario/), and the spouse claiming the exclusion has to prove that at the date of separation the gift property is traceable.

---

## 1. The Gift Letter, the Transfer Records, and a Separate Account. All Three Together

The gift letter shows the money was for you alone. The transfer records show the money actually came from your parents or any third party providing the gift. A separate account shows the money was never commingled with your family assets. Miss any one of the three, and the exclusion may not hold. Once the money is commingled with your other accounts, it is treated as a gift to the marriage and will be divided in [equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/).

The law does not list these specific requirements. It only requires that the money be traceable at the date of separation. The problem, in our practice, is that it is hard to prove before trial that the money is still clean and traceable. Many people [move the money between several accounts](/en/blog/mom-gift-moved-between-accounts-divorce-ontario/), sometimes chasing higher savings interest at different banks, sometimes simply because they are not organized, and in the end they cannot trace the money.

And there is one red line. The money cannot go toward the matrimonial home mortgage, and it cannot be used to renovate the matrimonial home. Once it goes into the matrimonial home, the exclusion is gone. The home's full value counts, and the gifted money cannot be pulled back out.

---

## 2. A Letter Each Time Is Better, but What Matters Most Is That the Money Can Still Be Traced Back

If your parents send money every year, it is better to have a gift letter for each payment, so every transfer has its own gift letter. But the letter is not decisive. What matters most is that on the date of separation, you can still trace the money all the way back, from the separation date to the day you received it, without it ever having been mixed with [living expenses](/en/blog/parents-money-child-account-living-expenses-divorce-ontario/) or family spending. If the trail holds, the money is yours. If it does not, letters alone will not be enough.

Furthermore, there is one tricky question. Do not use the money for your family expenses. Several cases say that if a person periodically receives money from parents and uses it not for investment but for daily expenses, such as mortgage payments, groceries, and living costs, the money can be deemed to be that person's income. The other party can then bring a motion to [impute income](/en/blog/imputed-income-child-support-ontario/). If your income is imputed at a high number, your [child support](/en/blog/imputed-income-child-support-ontario/) or [spousal support](/en/blog/imputed-income-spousal-support-ontario/) may reflect that high number.

So you need to be really careful about how you spend the money and how you manage your family finances after you receive the gift money. If possible, the better way is to have a [marriage contract](/en/blog/already-married-marriage-contract-home-down-payment-ontario/) with your spouse. Then you may not need to keep all the proof, and in the marriage contract you can easily exclude a specific amount of money.

---

## 3. No Formal Gift Letter? A WeChat Message May Work

A WeChat message may work. We have a successful case in which the judge recognized our client's [WeChat](/en/blog/electronic-evidence-ontario-family-court/) conversation and held that a specific transaction was a gift. If your parents said in WeChat that the money is for you alone, not for both of you, that message can be evidence of the purpose of the money. Ontario law looks at the real intention, and a written record is evidence of it.

However, if you are planning in advance, we would not recommend relying on chat messages. A conversation can change, and it can be interpreted in different ways. It is better to have a gift letter. That said, any written conversation, such as an email, a message, or WeChat, can be evidence before the court, and it is better than oral discussion alone.

But if you are worried the dispute might one day be litigated in China, a formal gift letter is safer. The process in China is different from Ontario's, and a clear formal gift letter is more readily recognized there.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Can I invest the money in the separate account?

Yes. Stocks, funds, or an investment property are all fine, as long as the investment still runs through that separate account and the chain stays unbroken. The only thing you cannot do is put the money into the matrimonial home.

### 2. What should the gift letter say?

Three things: who gave it, who received it, and that it is for one spouse alone, not for the couple. Include the amount and the date, and have your parents sign it. One letter per payment, each matching its transfer.

### 3. The money is already mixed with living expenses. Can it be fixed?

It is very hard. Once mixed, you cannot say which dollar is which, and the trail cannot be traced back at separation. What you can do is start separating now: open a new separate account, and have all future gifts from your parents go only into that account, never mixed again.
