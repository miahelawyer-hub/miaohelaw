Essentially, yes. One co-owner cannot sell the [whole jointly owned house](/en/blog/joint-title-spouse-sell-half-ontario/) alone. If your former partner will not agree to a sale and will not buy out your share either, the remaining route is court — an order for the house to be sold. His verbal refusal is not a legal veto. Once the house is sold, the net proceeds first go to pay off the mortgage and the costs of sale, and are then held in a lawyer's trust account until the two of you settle, or the court decides, how they are to be divided. If the matter drags on until the [bank forces a sale](/en/blog/mortgage-default-family-court-cannot-stop-bank-sale-ontario/), the bank's costs come off the top first, and what is left for the two of you is less.

A client came to consult me. She and her partner had [split up](/en/family-law/common-law/), and the house was in both their names. She wanted to sell it and divide the money. He would not agree to sell, and he would not buy out her share either; the situation was simply stuck. Her question: if he won't sign, can I never sell this house?

The short answer: you cannot sell it alone — but he cannot lock the house up forever either.

---

## 1. Selling the Whole House Requires Both Owners on Title

With the house in two names, a sale and transfer need the cooperation and signatures of both owners. You cannot complete a sale of the entire property by yourself, and no buyer or lawyer will close a transaction that cannot be transferred. So before there is an agreement or a court order, his refusal does block the sale.

---

## 2. His Verbal Refusal Is Not a Final Veto

Put the two routes in front of him. Route one: he [buys out your share](/en/blog/common-law-house-share-transfer-buyout-child-support-ontario/) at market value, takes over or refinances the mortgage, and completes the transfer — the house becomes his. Route two: the two of you list and sell the house together and divide the net proceeds. If he will take neither route and simply wants the stalemate to continue, the answer is to go to court and apply for partition or sale — an [order that the property be sold](/en/blog/force-sale-matrimonial-home-ontario/). The court looks at the facts of ownership: the house is jointly owned, the relationship has ended, and the two of you cannot go on holding it together. "I don't agree," said out loud, is not a legal reason to stop a sale. Once the order is made, his consent is no longer needed — the court has ordered the sale, and the sale must proceed under the order.

---

## 3. The Proceeds Are Not Divided the Day the Sale Closes

On closing, the mortgage is paid off first, and then the costs of sale — real estate commission, legal fees, and the like. The net proceeds that remain are ordinarily held in a lawyer's trust account, not paid out at once. They are released according to the outcome: a settlement between the two of you, or a court decision on the shares and the accounting. If, along the way, one of you has paid more than your share of the mortgage, property taxes, insurance, or repairs, or if there is a dispute about the ownership shares or occupation costs, that accounting is sorted out at this stage, before the money is divided.

---

## 4. The Outcome to Avoid Is a Forced Sale by the Bank

If the mortgage goes into default, the bank can start its own sale process. At that point, the mortgage debt, interest, the bank's legal costs, and the costs of sale all come out of the price first, and neither the method nor the price of the sale is in your hands. A house the two of you could have sold yourselves, sold instead under the bank's process, leaves less net money for both of you. So this cannot simply be left to drift: either reach a buyout or a sale by agreement, or start the court application promptly, and keep the house and the mortgage stable in the meantime.

---

## FAQs

### 1. He lives in the house, won't move out, and won't sign. Can he stall me forever?

He can stall for a while, not indefinitely. When you apply to court for a sale, you can also ask the court to set the sale arrangements and vacant possession. Once the order is made, the timetable for listing and for handing over the house follows the order. His living there does not make the house unsellable.

### 2. He says he wants to keep the house. Can he?

Yes — if he can actually buy it. Your share is valued at market price, he pays you out, and he deals with the mortgage and the transfer. Saying he wants to keep it, while neither buying you out nor allowing a sale, is not a position that can stand indefinitely.

### 3. Once the house is sold, is the money split half and half right away?

The mortgage and the costs of sale come off first; what remains is the net proceeds. The net proceeds are held in a lawyer's trust account and are divided at the end according to the ownership shares, your settlement, or the court's decision. If title is half and half and there are no other accounting disputes, the net proceeds are divided half and half at the end. If there are contributions, unequal shares, or occupation issues still in dispute, the accounting comes first, and the division after.
