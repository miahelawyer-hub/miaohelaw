Yes. Ontario's property division works from a photograph taken on the separation date: what he owned on that day, and what it was worth on that day, is locked in. Transferring the house afterward — even well below market value — changes where the house went, not the number on his side of the ledger. When the accounts are done, the house is still counted as his, at its separation-date value.

A client came to consult me. After the separation, her spouse had transferred a house out of his name at a low price, to someone cooperating with him. The house was no longer his on paper. Her question: does this house simply disappear from the division — do I get nothing from it?

The short answer: yes, it still counts. The separation-date value is locked in, and transferring the house away doesn't change the account.

---

## 1. The Separation Date Takes the Photograph, and the Accounting Starts From That Day

[Equalization](/en/blog/net-family-property-ontario/) in Ontario is based on each spouse's net family property, and net family property is measured on the [separation date](/en/blog/separation-date-vs-divorce-date-ontario/): the property he had that day is recorded at what it was worth that day. Where the house ended up afterward, and what has happened to it since, is a separate layer of the problem. So the first step in this accounting is not chasing the house — it is fixing its value on the separation date. If the value is disputed, an appraiser can value the property retrospectively, as of that date.

---

## 2. A Low-Price Transfer Changes the House's Destination, Not the Number

Whatever price he wrote on the transfer, and however close the recipient is to him, the house is still counted in the division as his property at its separation-date market value. When the final accounting is done, that value comes out of the share he keeps, or he makes it up to you in money. The transfer itself saves him nothing he was supposed to share — what he transferred away was the house, not the account.

---

## 3. The Accounting and Getting the House Back Are Two Different Things

A correct accounting settles what he owes you. The house itself is now in a third party's name, and making that third party return the house or the money is a separate fight — and a hard one, harder still if the property has changed hands again or the person is outside Ontario. In practice, most cases are resolved on the numbers: the amount is counted in, and it is made up out of other property or money, rather than the house itself being recovered. So put your effort in the right place: fix the separation-date value and the evidence first. Once the account stands, the money has something to attach to.

---

## 4. Whether He Has Other Property Decides How Dangerous This Is

If he has other property in his name, you don't need to worry much: once the accounting establishes what he owes you, it can be adjusted against, and enforced out of, his other assets — the money has somewhere to come from. The situation to watch is the opposite one: this house is his only property, and he has nothing else. Once that house is transferred away or destroyed, you can win the case outright and still collect nothing — a judgment is only a piece of paper, and if there is nothing left in his name, there is nothing to enforce against. In that situation, protecting yourself comes first: register a [matrimonial home designation](/en/blog/house-in-his-name-designate-matrimonial-home-ontario/), or a [notice](/en/blog/joint-title-spouse-sell-half-ontario/), on the house early, so he cannot deal with it without your consent. Lock the house down first; the accounting can be done slowly afterward. If the house is not locked down, an accounting that is perfectly correct can still turn out to be an empty one.

## FAQs

### 1. He transferred it at a price obviously below market. Will the court accept that low price?

No. The accounting uses market value at the separation date, not the figure he chose to write in the transfer. If the value is in dispute, an appraiser's retrospective appraisal as of the separation date, together with the municipal assessment and comparable sales from that period, will establish the number.

### 2. The house is worth much more now. Counting it at the separation-date value — don't I lose out?

It turns on [title](/en/blog/house-appreciation-after-separation-ontario/). The separation date locks in the figure on his side. If your name remained on title after separation, the increase in value can be claimed and shared. If the house was in his name alone and he transferred it away, the later increase is generally not yours to share. The photograph cuts both ways: if property in his name alone dropped in value after separation, that loss isn't deducted from your side either. What is locked is that one day.

### 3. He won't agree the house was worth that much on the separation date. What do I do?

Value isn't settled by arguing. Have an appraiser prepare a retrospective appraisal as of the separation date, supported by transaction records, the municipal assessment, and sales of comparable homes in the same area from that time. Whether the house is still in his name makes no difference to how its value is proved.
